Common Confusions About Free Will

These corrections identify distinctions, not winners.

If God knows it, God caused it

Knowledge and causation are different relations. The philosophical problem is whether infallibility plus the fixity of the past makes alternatives impossible, not whether a belief mechanically produces an act. See Foreknowledge and Free Will.

If an act is undetermined, it must be random

Indeterminism does not entail purposelessness, but neither does it by itself supply control. Agent-causal, event-causal, and non-causal libertarians offer different accounts of ownership. See Libertarian Free Will.

Compatibilists do not believe in free will

Compatibilists affirm free will while denying that determinism excludes it. Their critics dispute whether voluntariness, ownership, or reasons-responsiveness is enough. See the SEP entry.

Calvinists think people are robots

Reformed confessions affirm voluntary creaturely agency and describe effectual calling as renewing the will so that a person comes willingly. The dispute is whether this account provides the right control, not whether persons literally lack wills. See Westminster IX–X.

Arminians think humans save themselves

Arminius says free will cannot begin or perfect true spiritual good without grace; grace precedes, accompanies, and sustains response. See his “Grace and Free Will”.

Synergism means salvation is half God and half human

Synergy means real cooperation made possible and sustained by indispensable grace. It is not a percentage split or an assertion of equal independent causes. See Grace and Salvation.

Lutherans are Calvinists with sacraments

Both traditions teach monergistic conversion, but differ on universal grace, means of grace, election, resistance, baptism, and apostasy. Compare Lutheran and Reformed.

Catholicism has one theory of free will

Catholic doctrine sets boundaries while permitting Thomist/Bañezian, Molinist, and other accounts of efficacy and creaturely causation. See Roman Catholic.

The Church Fathers were all Arminian

Arminianism belongs to a post-Reformation controversy. Many Fathers are anti-fatalist or synergistic, but they address different questions in different vocabularies. See Church Fathers.

Augustine was simply Calvin before Calvin

Calvin receives Augustine within a different scriptural, medieval, ecclesial, and Reformation setting. Augustine's development and sacramental/catholic context cannot be erased. See Augustine's legacy.

Predestination and fatalism are identical

Fatalism says the outcome occurs regardless of deliberation and means. Predestinarian traditions ordinarily treat preaching, warning, faith, and action as means within providence. See Predestination.

Foreknowledge and predestination are identical

Foreknowledge concerns divine knowledge; predestination concerns divine purpose. Arminian, Molinist, Lutheran, Reformed, Catholic, and Orthodox accounts relate them differently. See Foreknowledge and Predestination.

Determinism is fatalism

Determinism says the past and laws fix the future. Fatalism says an outcome obtains regardless of what one does. A compatibilist treats deliberation and action as causally relevant parts of how the determined result occurs.
