# Glossary

Definitions are concise and comparative. Follow the links for disputes and tradition-specific uses.

## Agency and responsibility

### Free will

A significant kind of control over action, analyzed through alternatives, sourcehood, voluntariness, reasons, or their relation to responsibility. It is not one universally agreed faculty.

### Liberty

Freedom from a relevant constraint or freedom for self-directed action. Theological writers may call obedience to the good a higher liberty than indifferent choice.

### Determinism

The thesis that the complete past plus laws of nature fixes a single future. It is not [fatalism](#fatalism).

### Indeterminism

Denial that the relevant history and laws determine a unique outcome. Indeterminism alone does not establish agency or control.

### Fatalism

The thesis that an outcome will occur regardless of deliberation or action. In deterministic theories, by contrast, deliberation can be part of the causal route.

### Libertarianism

The conjunction of free will and incompatibilism: relevant freedom exists and cannot coexist with determinism. See [Philosophical Views](philosophical-views.md#libertarian-free-will).

### Compatibilism

The thesis that relevant freedom can coexist with determinism, often analyzed through voluntariness, ownership, or reasons-responsiveness.

### Incompatibilism

The thesis that determinism is incompatible with the relevant freedom. Libertarians and hard determinists are both incompatibilists.

### Hard determinism

Acceptance of determinism combined with denial of free will of the required kind.

### Hard incompatibilism

The view that neither deterministic nor indeterministic causation supplies the control needed for strong freedom or basic-desert responsibility.

### Free-will skepticism

A family of views denying, doubting, or suspending judgment about the kind of free will often thought to ground basic desert.

### Semi-compatibilism

The view that moral responsibility may be compatible with determinism even if the freedom to do otherwise is not.

### Sourcehood

The condition that an action originate from the agent in the right way, rather than merely occurring in or through them.

### Alternative possibilities

Genuinely accessible different actions or choices—often expressed as the ability to do otherwise.

### Principle of Alternative Possibilities

The proposed principle that a person is morally responsible only if they could have done otherwise. Frankfurt-style cases challenge it.

### Reasons-responsiveness

The capacity of an agent or action-producing mechanism to recognize and react appropriately to reasons across a suitable range.

### Agent causation

A libertarian proposal on which a substance or agent, not merely prior events, causes a free action in a distinctive way.

### Moral responsibility

An agent's eligibility for praise, blame, accountability, or other moral responses. Basic desert, attributability, and practical responsibility should be distinguished.

### Coercion

Threat or pressure that makes refusal unreasonably costly and can diminish responsibility without always removing intentional choice.

## Providence and knowledge

### Providence

Divine preservation and governance of creation, formulated differently through primary/secondary causation, concurrence, permission, middle knowledge, or other models.

### Foreknowledge

Knowledge of future events. Infallibility creates a modal problem about necessity, but knowledge is not identical to causation.

### Predestination

Divine purpose or appointment concerning salvation and, in some systems, the ordering of events. It is not identical to foreknowledge or determinism.

### Election

Divine choosing of a people or persons in Christ, interpreted as unconditional, conditional, corporate, sacramental/ecclesial, or in combinations.

### Reprobation

The non-election or condemnation of the lost, variously described as passing over, permission, judicial hardening, or a decree. The descriptions are not equivalent.

### Middle knowledge

In Molinism, God's logically pre-decretal knowledge of true counterfactuals of creaturely freedom, “middle” between natural and free knowledge.

### Counterfactuals of creaturely freedom

Claims about what a possible free creature *would* do in specified circumstances, central to Molinist providence.

## Sin, grace, and salvation

### Original sin

Western term for humanity's fallen condition and solidarity with Adam, including deprivation/corruption and, in several accounts, inherited guilt. Traditions define its elements differently.

### Ancestral sin

Common Eastern term emphasizing inherited mortality, corruption, and disordered conditions from Adam; its contrast with “original sin” should not be exaggerated into a universal East/West formula.

### Total inability

The claim that fallen people cannot turn to God savingly by natural power. It does not mean inability to reason or perform any civic good.

### Moral inability

Inability rooted in a contrary disposition or inclination rather than missing natural faculties or opportunity; especially important in Edwards.

### Grace

God's free favor, help, action, gift, or communicated life. The term's precise effect and metaphysics differ across traditions.

### Prevenient grace

Grace that goes before response. In Wesleyan theology it universally enables a saving response without making it inevitable.

### Sufficient grace

In Catholic debates, grace genuinely sufficient for a salutary act; schools dispute how it relates to efficacious grace and actual consent.

### Efficacious grace

Grace that actually brings about its intended saving effect. Thomist, Molinist, Augustinian, and Reformed uses differ.

### Irresistible grace

Common Reformed name for effectual calling that renews the will and infallibly results in willing faith; not normally external coercion.

### Resistible grace

Grace that a person can reject. The claim must specify the kind or stage of grace.

### Regeneration

New birth or divine renewal giving spiritual life. Traditions relate it differently to faith, baptism, justification, and conversion.

### Monergism

The claim that God alone accomplishes regeneration/conversion in the defining salvific sense. Lutheran and Reformed versions differ.

### Synergism

Real human cooperation with indispensable divine grace. It does not mean equal causal shares or unaided merit.

### Justification

God's act of making or declaring sinners righteous. Protestant traditions emphasize forensic imputation; Catholic and Orthodox accounts integrate forgiveness and transformative participation in different ways.

### Sanctification

Growth or consecration in holiness by the Spirit, including transformed desire, virtue, action, and communion with God.

### Perseverance

Continuance in saving faith to the end. Reformed doctrine grounds certain perseverance in preservation; other traditions make final apostasy possible.

### Apostasy

Final abandonment or repudiation of faith and saving communion, distinguished from temporary doubt or serious sin followed by restoration.

### Theosis

Participation by grace in divine life—becoming by grace what God is by nature without identity of essence—central in Eastern Christian soteriology.

## Historical schools and controversies

### Pelagianism

A cluster of early-fifth-century positions contested by Augustine concerning sin, grace, and ability. The label should not be used for every synergistic view.

### Semi-Pelagianism

A later label for positions assigning the beginning of faith to unaided will while requiring grace for progress. Its application to Cassian is disputed.

### Augustinianism

Traditions drawing especially on Augustine's mature doctrines of original sin, grace, election, and perseverance; Catholic, Lutheran, and Reformed receptions are not identical.

### Thomism

The theological/philosophical school interpreting Thomas Aquinas, including real secondary causation, grace, and predestination. It contains internal debates.

### Bañezianism

A Thomist school associated with Domingo Báñez that stresses physical premotion and intrinsically efficacious grace while defending voluntary action.

### Molinism

The school derived from Luis de Molina that uses middle knowledge to coordinate providence, predestination, grace, and libertarian creaturely freedom.

### Arminianism

A tradition deriving from Jacobus Arminius and later developments that affirms inability without grace, resistible enabling grace, and conditional election.

### Wesleyanism

The Arminian tradition shaped by John Wesley, emphasizing universal prevenient grace, new birth, assurance, sanctification/perfect love, and possible apostasy.

## Maximus on willing

### Natural will

In Maximus, the rational appetite or capacity for the good belonging to human nature. Christ has a complete human natural will.

### Gnomic will

In Maximus's mature usage, a deliberative mode of willing associated with uncertainty and a person's manner of using natural powers. It is not simply a separate faculty, and attributing it to Christ is rejected.
