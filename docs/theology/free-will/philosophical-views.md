# Philosophical Views

Contemporary philosophy distinguishes a claim about the truth of determinism from a claim about what freedom or responsibility requires. “Compatibilist” and “incompatibilist” identify positions on that relation; they do not by themselves describe a theology of grace.

## A map of the debate

```text
Is the relevant freedom compatible with determinism?
├── Yes: compatibilism
└── No: incompatibilism
    ├── Freedom exists: libertarianism
    └── Freedom does not exist or is doubtful
        ├── hard determinism
        ├── hard incompatibilism
        └── free-will skepticism
```

## Libertarian free will

Libertarians affirm meaningful free will and hold that it is incompatible with causal determinism. Many require alternative possibilities; source libertarians place the emphasis on the agent as the appropriate origin of action.

Major families include:

- **Agent-causal theories:** the agent, not merely an event in the agent, causes a choice in a distinctive way.
- **Event-causal theories:** indeterministic relations among beliefs, desires, efforts, and decisions explain action without a special substance-causal power.
- **Non-causal theories:** a basic action or decision can be purposive without being caused by prior events.

> **Indeterminism alone does not produce freedom.**

If chance simply interrupts a causal chain, it is unclear why the result belongs more fully to the agent. The *luck objection* therefore presses libertarians to explain enhanced control, rational explanation, and ownership—not merely openness.

See also [Alternative Possibilities](dimensions.md#alternative-possibilities) and the SEP survey of [incompatibilist theories](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/incompatibilism-theories/).

## Compatibilism

Compatibilism holds that determinism and the relevant freedom can coexist. Classical versions focus on voluntary action without external constraint: the agent does what they want to do. Contemporary versions add hierarchical identification, ownership of motives, guidance control, or reasons-responsiveness.

Compatibilists need not deny every meaningful “could have done otherwise.” Some use conditional or dispositional analyses; others argue that responsibility depends primarily on how the actual sequence issued from the agent. Frankfurt-style cases motivate the latter approach.

The main objections include:

- **Manipulation:** if an agent's values were engineered, acting from them may not be enough.
- **Consequence:** if the past and laws are not up to us, how can their fixed consequences be up to us?
- **Sourcehood:** does a deterministic history leave the agent sufficiently ultimate or authentic?

See the [SEP entry on compatibilism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/compatibilism/) and [Moral Responsibility](moral-responsibility.md).

## Incompatibilism

Incompatibilism is the umbrella thesis that determinism excludes the relevant kind of freedom. It does not entail belief in free will: libertarians affirm freedom, whereas hard determinists and hard incompatibilists deny or doubt it.

## Hard determinism

Hard determinists accept determinism and conclude that free will of the required kind does not exist. The position must then revise or reject practices grounded in basic desert, though it may preserve deterrence, moral education, protection, and interpersonal expectations.

## Hard incompatibilism

Hard incompatibilists argue that neither deterministic nor indeterministic processes give agents the control needed for basic-desert responsibility. The problem is therefore deeper than determinism: an action produced by chance may be no more ultimately controlled than one fixed by prior causes.

## Free-will skepticism

Skepticism is a broader family of views holding that strong free will is absent, incoherent, or insufficiently justified. A skeptic may remain agnostic about determinism. Skeptics differ on whether responsibility practices should be retained, reformed, or abandoned.

## Semi-compatibilism

Semi-compatibilism, associated especially with John Martin Fischer, holds that moral responsibility may be compatible with determinism even if the freedom to do otherwise is not. The proposal separates *regulative control* (access to alternatives) from *guidance control* (the actual action issuing through the agent's own suitably reasons-responsive mechanism).

## Comparison

| Position | Determinism compatible with freedom? | Affirms free will? | Alternatives required? | Recurring challenge |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Libertarianism | No | Yes | Usually, though source accounts vary | Luck, randomness, and control |
| Compatibilism | Yes | Yes | Not necessarily | Manipulation and sourcehood |
| Hard determinism | No | No | No | Reconstructing responsibility |
| Hard incompatibilism | No adequate freedom either way | Usually no | No | Agency and practical life without desert |
| Free-will skepticism | Doubtful or irrelevant | No or agnostic | No | Scope of revision to moral practices |
| Semi-compatibilism | Responsibility may be | Varies | No for responsibility | Ground of guidance control |

“Free will?” and “moral responsibility?” remain distinguishable questions. The [SEP discussion of incompatibilist arguments](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/incompatibilism-arguments/) explicitly cautions against collapsing them.

## Reformed Assessment

Libertarianism's strongest contribution is its insistence on creaturely sourcehood and genuine alternatives. From a confessional Reformed perspective, its principal costs are that indeterminacy does not itself explain enhanced control, alternative possibilities may not be necessary for responsibility, and undetermined creaturely decisions fit comprehensive providence less naturally. Compatibilism better supports voluntary responsibility under providence, but must answer manipulation and ultimate-sourcehood objections. Hard determinism and skepticism are also rejected because they thin responsible personal agency rather than locating it within purposeful divine and secondary causation.

See [A Reformed Evaluation of Free Will](reformed-evaluation.md#why-libertarian-freedom-is-weaker) for the full, intentionally confessional argument.
