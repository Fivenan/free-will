# A Reformed Evaluation of Free Will

> This page evaluates the major free-will and grace traditions from a confessional Reformed perspective. Historical descriptions elsewhere in this wiki remain descriptive; the analysis here is intentionally theological and evaluative.

The Reformed account is presented here as the strongest synthesis because it preserves comprehensive divine sovereignty, radical dependence on grace, meaningful human voluntariness and responsibility, unconditional election, effectual calling, and perseverance without making salvation finally depend on an autonomous human decision.

This is a cumulative argument, not a claim that Reformed theology has no hard questions. Its governing comparison is **explanatory cost**: what must each model affirm, qualify, or leave unresolved in order to hold together its account of providence, agency, sin, grace, and final salvation?

## Criteria for Evaluation

These are **confessional Reformed theological criteria**, not religiously neutral axioms. A reader who rejects the Reformed doctrine of Scripture, providence, or sin will predictably rank the models differently.

1. Does the position preserve the biblical depiction of God's comprehensive sovereignty?
2. Does it take fallen human inability seriously?
3. Does it make divine grace genuinely decisive in salvation?
4. Does it preserve meaningful human responsibility?
5. Does it distinguish voluntariness from libertarian indeterminacy?
6. Does it adequately explain election and predestination?
7. Does it account for texts in which divine ordination and human intention operate simultaneously?
8. Does it avoid making the decisive difference between believer and unbeliever rest finally in autonomous self-determination?
9. Does it give a coherent relation among regeneration, faith, and perseverance?
10. Does it maintain God's holiness while affirming comprehensive providence?
11. Does it explain the relevant texts with fewer ad hoc qualifications than competing systems?
12. Is its philosophical account of agency internally coherent?

## The Reformed Account

### Comprehensive providence

God does not merely observe a history constituted independently of divine purpose. God creates, sustains, concurs with, and governs creaturely causes, while those creatures remain real secondary causes. [Westminster Confession V](https://www.opc.org/wcf.html) therefore joins meticulous providence to creaturely contingency and denies that God is the author or approver of sin; [Belgic Confession 13](https://www.crcna.org/welcome/beliefs/confessions/belgic-confession) makes the same holiness qualification.

Genesis 50:20, Isaiah 10, Acts 2:23, and Acts 4:27–28 display the characteristic pattern: **the same event may be ordained by God for a holy purpose and performed by human agents for sinful purposes**. Divine and creaturely intentions are concurrent, but they are neither morally identical nor rival causes on the same level.

### Human voluntariness

Reformed theology does not require a free action to be causally undetermined. It requires the action to proceed voluntarily from the person's own desires, intentions, character, and reasoning rather than from external coercion. Determination and coercion are therefore different claims. The remaining philosophical dispute is whether voluntariness, ownership, and reasons-responsiveness provide enough sourcehood for moral desert.

### Fallen inability

The account distinguishes three things:

- possession of ordinary human faculties;
- voluntary deliberation and choice;
- moral and spiritual ability to love God rightly.

Fallen people retain mind and will, yet do not generate saving faith from an unrenewed disposition. [Belgic Confession 14](https://www.crcna.org/welcome/beliefs/confessions/belgic-confession) and [Westminster Confession IX](https://www.opc.org/wcf.html) therefore deny saving ability without denying human agency. Jonathan Edwards's natural/moral inability distinction is a later analytical expression of this pattern, not the definition of every Reformed confession.

### Effectual grace

```text
fallen sinner
    ↓
effectual calling / regeneration
    ↓
renewed disposition
    ↓
willing faith and repentance
```

God does not force an unwilling believer to believe; God renews the heart so that the person willingly comes. [Westminster Confession X](https://www.opc.org/wcf.html), [Westminster Larger Catechism 67](https://www.opc.org/confessions.html), and [Canons of Dort III/IV.11–14](https://www.crcna.org/welcome/beliefs/confessions/canons-dort) make grace causally productive of both the ability and act of faith. The regenerated person is active in believing; monergism identifies the source of the renewing act, not the absence of the human response.

### Unconditional election

Election rests on God's gracious purpose rather than foreseen merit or autonomous faith. Faith is the ordained effect and instrument of salvation, not the independent condition that explains why one person enters the elect company. The [Canons of Dort I](https://www.crcna.org/welcome/beliefs/confessions/canons-dort) and [Belgic Confession 16](https://www.crcna.org/welcome/beliefs/confessions/belgic-confession) state this while also locating judgment in human sin.

### Perseverance

Final salvation rests on divine preservation, exercised through faith, warning, prayer, sacrament, discipline, and other appointed means. Perseverance is not confidence in the permanence of an autonomous choice. [Westminster Confession XVII](https://www.opc.org/wcf.html) and [Canons of Dort V](https://www.crcna.org/welcome/beliefs/confessions/canons-dort) also acknowledge serious falls, disrupted assurance, and the real efficacy of warnings.

## A Cumulative Case

```text
comprehensive providence
        +
radical fallen inability
        +
decisive divine grace
        +
voluntary human agency
        +
unconditional election
        +
effectual calling
        +
perseverance
        =
a unified Reformed account
```

The argument does not depend on Calvin's authority alone. Its force is the proposed mutual support among these doctrines: providence supplies a framework for concurrent agency; inability establishes the need for causally effective grace; effectual calling explains willing faith; election identifies grace's gracious source; and preservation carries the same divine initiative to completion.

## A Biblical Case with Exegetical Hinges

The passages below are not detached proof texts. Each is presented with a serious alternative and the point at which interpretation turns.

### Genesis 50:20; Isaiah 10; Acts 2:23; Acts 4:27–28

#### Reformed reading

Joseph's brothers intend evil while God intends the same action for good. Assyria acts arrogantly while serving a divine purpose it does not share. Jesus is crucified by culpable agents according to God's plan, and Acts 4 describes their acts as predestined. Together these passages supply a pattern of divine-human concurrence.

#### Major alternative reading

A libertarian providential reading can treat these as specially governed redemptive events: God foreknows, permits, limits, and incorporates wicked choices without causally determining every intention. Exceptional predetermination need not establish a general compatibilist account.

#### Exegetical hinge

The dispute turns on whether the texts attribute only foreknown permission and subsequent use to God, or a prior intention and appointment encompassing the creaturely event; it also turns on how representative these events are of providence generally.

#### Reformed assessment

The verbs of intention, plan, and predestination make bare permission too thin, while the texts' direct blame of human agents excludes fatalism. The concurrence reading therefore explains both claims without making divine purpose and creaturely culpability compete.

### Ezekiel 36:26–27 and John 6:37–45

#### Reformed reading

God removes the heart of stone, gives a new heart, and causes covenant obedience; those given by the Father come to the Son, and the Father's drawing succeeds in resurrection on the last day. Renewal explains the willing response rather than merely restoring neutrality.

#### Major alternative reading

Arminian and Wesleyan readings understand enabling grace as prior and indispensable but resistible. Ezekiel may describe corporate covenant restoration, while John 6 may describe drawing through revelation that can be refused and must be read alongside broader invitations.

#### Exegetical hinge

The decisive questions are whether heart-renewal causes the faithful response, whether the giving and drawing in John 6 are coextensive with those who come and are raised, and how the immediate audience and wider canonical invitations determine scope.

#### Reformed assessment

The promised transformation and the linked sequence of giving, coming, and raising fit effectual renewal more directly than a universally restored but indeterminate capacity. The alternative preserves universal enablement, but must supply that universality from other texts and systematic inference.

### Romans 8:28–30; Romans 9; Ephesians 1–2; Acts 13:48

#### Reformed reading

God's purpose links foreknowledge, predestination, calling, justification, and glorification; Romans 9 grounds mercy in God's call rather than willing or running; Ephesians locates election before creation and faith within grace; Acts relates appointment to life and belief. Election is particular, unconditional, and causally integrated with calling and faith.

#### Major alternative reading

Conditional and corporate readings understand election primarily in Christ and identify the elect body through faith. “Foreknown” may name foreseen believers or covenant relationship; Romans 9 may focus on historical vocation and the constitution of God's people rather than an exhaustive order of individual salvation. Arminius's [*Declaration of Sentiments*](https://ccel.org/ccel/arminius/works1/works1.iii.html) is an important primary example of a Christ-centered, conditional construction.

#### Exegetical hinge

Interpretation turns on the objects of foreknowledge and election, the role of individuals within the corporate argument, whether calling produces the saving response, and whether Paul's “not by works” contrast also excludes foreseen faith as election's ground.

#### Reformed assessment

No single verse settles every later question, but the cumulative order from purpose through calling to faith is more naturally causal than reactive. Corporate election is real, yet does not by itself explain why particular people are savingly incorporated.

### 1 Timothy 2:1–6 and 2 Peter 3:8–10

#### Reformed reading

These texts ground indiscriminate prayer and gospel proclamation and reveal God's real benevolence. Reformed interpreters variously understand “all” as all kinds of people, distinguish God's preceptive or revealed will from the decree, and read 2 Peter's patience in relation to its addressed “beloved.” No faithful Reformed reading may turn the invitations into insincere language.

#### Major alternative reading

Arminian, Wesleyan, Lutheran, Catholic, and Orthodox readers commonly take the passages as affirming God's saving desire for every individual and therefore as pressure against a decree of particular effectual grace.

#### Exegetical hinge

The dispute turns on the scope of “all,” the addressees of each letter, whether Scripture may speak of divine will in more than one sense, and how these texts relate canonically to particular-election passages.

#### Reformed assessment

The universal reading is a serious objection, not a verbal mistake. Yet a universal saving desire does not by itself establish universal libertarian enablement or settle how God's revealed compassion relates to redemptive purpose. The Reformed distinctions are defensible when they arise from context and the whole canon rather than being invoked only to protect a system.

### Hebrews 6:4–12 and Hebrews 10:19–39

#### Reformed reading

The warnings address the covenant community and are means by which God preserves believers. Reformed interpreters differ over the precise status of those described, but commonly distinguish profound participation in covenant blessings from the regenerating union that certainly perseveres.

#### Major alternative reading

Lutheran, Wesleyan, Catholic, and Orthodox readings can take the descriptions as referring to genuine believers who may finally repudiate grace. The [Formula of Concord XI](https://bookofconcord.org/solid-declaration/election/) and Wesley's [“On Working Out Our Own Salvation”](https://wesley.nnu.edu/john-wesley/the-sermons-of-john-wesley-1872-edition/sermon-85-on-working-out-our-own-salvation/) represent traditions in which warning and possible apostasy belong together.

#### Exegetical hinge

The questions are whether the experiential descriptions entail regeneration, how the agricultural analogy in Hebrews 6 functions, and whether a divinely effective warning can describe a danger that God uses to prevent among the elect.

#### Reformed assessment

The apostasy reading gives the warning language its most immediate force. Reformed perseverance remains coherent, however, if warnings are real instruments rather than predictions that the regenerate will finally fall. The status of the apostates remains one of the framework's most disputed exegetical questions.

## Why Libertarian Freedom Is Weaker

Libertarian freedom seeks to preserve genuine alternatives and strong creaturely sourcehood. It argues that, for at least some morally significant actions, the agent must be able to choose otherwise under the same relevant conditions. Agent-causal, event-causal, and non-causal versions should not be collapsed.

### Weakness 1: indeterminacy does not explain control

The luck objection asks: **what makes an undetermined choice more genuinely mine rather than merely less determined?** Libertarian theories answer with agent causation, purposive effort, or indeterministic reasons-sensitive processes; the [SEP survey](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/incompatibilism-theories/) treats these as substantive replies. Yet every version still owes an account of why the actual outcome expresses enhanced control when the same prior conditions leave another outcome open. Indeterminacy is not identical to randomness, but it is not yet an explanation of ownership.

### Weakness 2: alternatives may not be necessary for responsibility

Frankfurt-style cases, voluntariness, actual-sequence sourcehood, and reasons-responsiveness make it plausible that responsibility tracks what a person knowingly intends and loves, even when a contrary option is unavailable. The cases remain disputed, as the [SEP discussion of compatibilism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/compatibilism/) notes, but libertarianism cannot simply assume the Principle of Alternative Possibilities.

### Weakness 3: strong libertarianism raises a providence cost

If decisive creaturely choices are categorically undetermined by divine providence, the Reformed account judges them difficult to integrate with the scriptural concurrence pattern. Molinism offers world-selection through middle knowledge, but transfers the cost to pre-decretal counterfactuals and their grounding.

### Weakness 4: libertarianism does not solve spiritual inability

Even if libertarian agency describes some ordinary choices, it does not establish that fallen people can love God savingly. A further doctrine of enabling grace is still required.

**Reformed conclusion:** libertarianism incurs substantial metaphysical costs without being necessary to explain moral responsibility or biblical divine-human concurrence.

## Why the Arminian Solution Is Weaker

Arminian theology is not Pelagianism. Arminius denies saving ability apart from grace; grace precedes, accompanies, and sustains response, remains resistible, and election is commonly related to foreknown faith. His own account of final apostasy was cautious, while later Remonstrants developed it further. See Arminius's [“Grace and Free Will”](https://ccel.org/ccel/arminius/works2/works2.ix.vi.html) and the [1610 Remonstrance](https://arminiusinstituut.remonstranten.nl/wp-content/uploads/sites/32/2017/08/Remonstrantie-Orginal-Act-English.pdf).

### Weakness 1: the decisive-difference problem

If two equally fallen sinners receive genuinely sufficient enabling grace, and one believes while the other resists, what finally explains the difference? The Arminian reply is that neither response is ungraced, faith merits nothing, and a personal response need not compete with grace. The Reformed concern remains causal rather than meritorious: if grace supplies equal sufficient ability while the undetermined assent differentiates the outcomes, grace is necessary but not finally decisive at the point of difference.

### Weakness 2: election can appear reactive

Conditional election protects the priority of Christ and excludes merit. Yet when foreseen faith is election's condition, election recognizes the successful response rather than explaining why saving grace produces it.

### Weakness 3: universal enabling grace bears a large exegetical burden

Universal prevenient or enabling grace is systematically coherent, but the Reformed reading disputes that Scripture clearly teaches a universal restoration of libertarian saving ability. The doctrine often functions as the bridge between universal invitation, total inability, and resistible faith; each of those concerns is textually motivated, but the bridge itself requires cumulative inference.

### Weakness 4: perseverance becomes contingent

Where final apostasy is possible, final salvation depends in part on continued non-resistance. Arminianism remains grace-centered, but from the Reformed standpoint it does not make grace decisive at the final explanatory point.

## Why the Wesleyan Solution Is Weaker

Wesleyanism strongly affirms total inability apart from grace, universal prevenient grace, resistible response, assurance, sanctification, and commonly conditional security. The [United Methodist doctrinal standards](https://www.umc.org/en/content/foundational-documents) and Wesley's sermons should control its representation.

### Weakness 1: prevenient grace carries major explanatory weight

Universal prevenient grace must both answer inability and restore response without determining it. The Reformed question is whether this universal restoration is directly taught or is principally a systematic inference joining universal invitation to libertarian response.

### Weakness 2: the decisive-difference problem remains

If grace restores genuine saving ability to all, the differing response finally distinguishes belief from unbelief. Wesleyans answer that every good impulse remains graced and faith is not meritorious; the Reformed critique is again about explanatory efficacy, not earning.

### Weakness 3: apostasy complicates perseverance and assurance

Wesleyan assurance is real and Spirit-given. Nevertheless, if a regenerate person may finally fall, present assurance does not include the same ground of certain divine preservation affirmed at Dort.

### Strength acknowledged

Wesleyanism preserves the priority of grace, universal gospel invitation, meaningful response, and sustained attention to holy love. The Reformed account judges effectual grace stronger because the same grace that initiates renewal also explains faith and final perseverance.

## Why Catholic Accounts Are Weaker from a Reformed Perspective

Roman Catholicism is not one metaphysical theory. It rejects Pelagianism, affirms divine initiative and necessary grace, and contains sophisticated accounts of divine causation. Thomist/Bañezian approaches, Molinism, and the broader Catholic doctrine of grace must be distinguished.

### Weakness 1: the confessional disagreement over justification

The [Catechism on grace and justification](https://www.vatican.va/content/catechism/en/part_three/section_one/chapter_three/article_2/grace_and_justification.html) and [Trent, Session VI](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Canons_and_Decrees_of_the_Council_of_Trent/Session_VI/Justification) describe initial justification as gratuitous while including interior renewal, free grace-enabled cooperation, sacramental life, and the possibility of grace-enabled merit. The Reformed confessions distinguish justification from sanctification and ground acceptance in Christ's imputed righteousness received through faith alone. The central weakness, under Reformed criteria, is not an absence of grace but a soteriology that does not preserve *sola gratia* and justification in the same confessional sense.

### Weakness 2: Molinism depends on controversial middle knowledge

Molinism combines libertarian freedom and meticulous providence through God's pre-decretal knowledge of true counterfactuals of creaturely freedom. Its explanatory cost is the grounding question: what makes those counterfactuals true independently of God's decree and the creature's actual decision? The [SEP providence survey](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/providence-divine/) presents the proposal and major objections.

### Weakness 3: Thomism nears strong divine causality but differs in soteriology

Thomist/Bañezian motion is metaphysically closer to strong divine causation than popular “Catholic free will” summaries suggest. The deeper Reformed disagreements concern justification, merit, sacramental theology, perseverance, and ecclesiology. Thomism is therefore neither Molinism nor Reformed theology.

**Reformed conclusion:** Catholic theology has formidable metaphysical resources, but it does not preserve the same confessional account of *sola gratia* and justification.

## Why the Orthodox Synergistic Account Is Weaker

Eastern Orthodoxy treats grace as indispensable divine life and action, freedom as healed, salvation as participation and theosis, and synergy as real grace-sustained cooperation—not a 50/50 transaction. John of Damascus and the [Orthodox Church in America](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith) provide representative sources, though Orthodoxy has no single modern analytic theory.

### Weakness 1: synergy leaves the decisive-response question open

The relational and participatory model explains what salvation is, but if grace may finally be resisted, the same question recurs: what ultimately differentiates cooperation from resistance? Saying that the person truly participates protects agency but does not yet identify why grace reaches its saving effect in one case.

### Weakness 2: anthropology of the Fall differs

Orthodox sources commonly emphasize inherited mortality and corruption rather than inherited guilt and Reformed moral inability. This is neither Pelagianism nor denial of grace. From the Reformed framework, however, it understates the depth of inability expressed in John 6, Romans 8, and Ephesians 2 and therefore reduces the need for effectual renewal.

### Weakness 3: election texts carry less causal weight

Orthodox readings commonly integrate election with foreknowledge, Christ, and the divine desire to save without giving unconditional individual election the Reformed causal role. Reformed readers judge Romans 8–9, Ephesians 1, and Acts 13:48 more naturally explained by particular election and effectual calling.

### Weakness 4: categories are weighted differently

The absence of a Reformed *ordo salutis* is not by itself an intellectual defect; Orthodoxy organizes salvation around participation, sacrament, and theosis. The narrower assessment is that, under the stated Reformed criteria, the Orthodox model gives less explanatory force to unconditional election and effectual calling.

## Why Lutheranism Comes Close but Remains Weaker

Confessional Lutheranism strongly affirms bondage of the fallen will, monergistic conversion through Word and sacrament, universal grace, and rejection of Pelagian cooperation. The [Formula of Concord II](https://bookofconcord.org/solid-declaration/free-will/) says conversion is wholly the Spirit's work, while Lutheran doctrine also affirms resistance and possible apostasy.

### Tension 1: resistible grace plus monergistic conversion

If conversion is wholly God's work, why does the same grace result in conversion in one case and final resistance in another? Lutheran theology often refuses to infer beyond what it judges Scripture has revealed and locates blame wholly in the resistor; appeal to mystery is not irrationality. Reformed theology nevertheless considers unconditional election and effectual calling a more systematic resolution because divine grace explains the positive difference.

### Tension 2: apostasy

Possible loss of regeneration means the grace that monergistically creates faith does not ensure completion. Within the Reformed framework, preservation is more consistent with effectual renewal, while warnings remain divinely appointed means.

### Tension 3: election and non-election

[Formula of Concord XI](https://bookofconcord.org/solid-declaration/election/) teaches election to salvation in Christ and refuses a symmetrical decree, tracing condemnation to sin and resistance. Reformed confessions agree on asymmetrical culpability but integrate non-election within a more comprehensive decree. The Reformed gain is systematic integration; its added burden is the author-of-sin objection.

**Reformed conclusion:** Lutheranism is among the closest alternatives, but it is less systematic where monergistic grace, resistibility, election, and apostasy intersect.

## Why Hard Determinism and Free-Will Skepticism Are Weaker

Reformed theology is not “secular hard determinism plus God.” It affirms personal agency, rational deliberation, voluntariness, moral responsibility, purposeful providence, and layered primary and secondary causation. Hard determinism and strong responsibility skepticism gain causal simplicity at the cost of a sufficiently rich account of responsible personal agency. Skeptical models can preserve forward-looking protection and formation, but revision of basic-desert responsibility is precisely what Reformed moral theology rejects. See the [SEP overview of responsibility skepticism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/skepticism-moral-responsibility/).

## A Brief Reformed Assessment of Open Theism

Open theism protects providential responsiveness by holding that some future free acts are not yet definite truths. From the Reformed perspective, this adds greater costs than classical Arminianism: exhaustive definite foreknowledge, the specificity of prophecy, and confidence in comprehensive providence all require revision. Open theists affirm omniscience as knowledge of reality exactly as it is, so “God guesses” is a caricature; the disagreement concerns whether future free actions are definite objects of knowledge. See the [SEP foreknowledge survey](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/free-will-foreknowledge/).

## Comparative Explanatory Costs

| View | Main strength | Principal weakness from a Reformed perspective |
|---|---|---|
| Libertarianism | Strong alternatives and creaturely sourcehood | Indeterminacy does not itself secure control and fits comprehensive providence less naturally |
| Arminianism | Universal invitation, prior grace, and resistible response | Enabling grace does not finally explain why one person believes |
| Wesleyanism | Developed universal prevenient grace and sanctification | Prevenient grace carries large exegetical and explanatory weight |
| Molinism | Sophisticated reconciliation of providence and libertarian freedom | Grounding and providence problems surround middle knowledge |
| Catholic Thomism | Strong divine causality and secondary causes | Different doctrine of justification and unresolved internal freedom debates |
| Eastern Orthodox synergy | Relational, participatory account of salvation | Less compatible with Reformed readings of inability, election, and effectual calling |
| Lutheranism | Monergistic conversion and strong means-of-grace theology | Tension among monergism, resistibility, election, and apostasy |
| Hard determinism / skepticism | Strong causal or revisionary critique | Thin account of moral responsibility and theological agency |
| Open theism | Strong account of responsive divine-human relations | Revises exhaustive definite foreknowledge and weakens providential certainty |
| Reformed compatibilism | Integrates providence, grace, and responsibility | Must answer author-of-sin, evil, manipulation, and ultimate-sourcehood objections |

## Why the Reformed Model Is Stronger

1. **Grace is decisive rather than merely enabling.** God's regenerating grace, not an autonomous difference in response, explains saving faith.
2. **Providence is comprehensive.** One concurrence framework covers ordinary causes, sinful acts, the crucifixion, conversion, and perseverance.
3. **Human willing remains real.** Determination is not coercion; people deliberate and act from their own reasons and loves.
4. **Fallen inability is treated consistently.** Regeneration does more than return a sinner to metaphysical neutrality; it renews the disposition.
5. **Election is integrated with grace.** Election explains why grace reaches saving effect rather than recognizing a future response.
6. **Perseverance is grounded in divine preservation.** The God who begins renewal completes it through appointed means.
7. **Libertarian metaphysics is unnecessary.** Voluntariness, actual-sequence sourcehood, and reasons-responsiveness do the required agency work with fewer metaphysical commitments.
8. **Scripture contains strong examples of concurrent agency.** Joseph and his brothers, Assyria, and the crucifixion join divine purpose to responsible creaturely intention without moral equivalence.

From a confessional Reformed standpoint, the principal advantage is not one isolated doctrine, but the way providence, sin, grace, election, voluntary agency, and perseverance mutually reinforce one another.

## The Hardest Questions for the Reformed Position

### God and the ordination of evil

If every event falls within providence, why is God not the author of sin? Reformed confessions distinguish God's holy ordination and concurrence from the creature's defective intention, deny that divine causation is morally symmetrical with creaturely causation, and invoke secondary causes. That is a coherent distinction, but the problem of evil demands more than repeating it: the account must show that God has morally sufficient purposes and does not inject evil motives into agents.

### Ultimate sourcehood

If a person's character and desires are ultimately within a determined order, is the person a sufficient source for basic desert? Compatibilist replies appeal to ownership, rational agency, and action flowing from the person's own evaluative standpoint. Manipulation arguments press whether that source is deep enough. This is probably the strongest philosophical objection to the model.

### Universal-saving-will passages

First Timothy 2 and 2 Peter 3 appear to express a broad divine desire to save. Reformed distinctions among decree, precept, benevolence, and all kinds of people must be contextually demonstrated rather than used as escape clauses. The universal readings remain important evidence against particular election.

### Love and effectual grace

Does transforming desire preserve meaningful relational freedom, or would love require the live possibility of final refusal? The Reformed reply is that healed love can be most free when it is most fully inclined to the good; God's work establishes willing communion rather than bypassing it. A libertarian will dispute whether transformed willingness is enough.

These objections are serious, but need not be fatal if concurrent agency is coherent, voluntariness grounds responsibility, and the scriptural case for effectual grace and election is cumulative. The comparative claim is that rival systems face greater or less manageable costs—not that Reformed theology has no mysteries.

## Conclusion

The competing traditions preserve genuine biblical and theological concerns, especially the seriousness of human response, the universality of gospel proclamation, and relational language about grace. From a confessional Reformed perspective, however, they generally preserve these concerns at a greater explanatory cost: grace becomes enabling rather than finally decisive, providence becomes less comprehensive, election becomes conditioned or less causally central, or perseverance becomes contingent on continued human response.

The Reformed account is therefore judged strongest because it makes grace finally decisive, preserves comprehensive providence and voluntary responsibility, takes fallen inability seriously, grounds election in God's purpose, treats regeneration as productive of faith, grounds perseverance in divine preservation, avoids requiring libertarian indeterminacy, and offers a unified account of divine and human agency. That conclusion remains conditional on sustaining its compatibilist account of responsibility and its distinction between divine ordination and divine moral approval of evil. This is intentionally a Reformed conclusion.
