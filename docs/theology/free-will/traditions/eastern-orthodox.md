# Eastern Orthodox

> **Descriptive:** This page summarizes representative Orthodox emphases without imposing a single modern analytic system. The section labeled “Reformed Assessment” changes explicitly to a confessional Reformed judgment.

## Summary

Eastern Orthodox theology commonly presents freedom within creation in God's image, ancestral sin, sacramental healing, synergy, and theosis. It strongly affirms divine initiative and genuine response but does not define itself through modern libertarian/compatibilist categories.

## Human nature and the Fall

“Ancestral sin” commonly emphasizes mortality, corruption, disordered passions, and solidarity with Adam. Orthodox writers vary in how they discuss inherited culpability and Western “original sin.” Human nature remains God's good creation but needs liberation and healing in Christ.

## Grace

Grace is the uncreated divine life and action by which human beings are healed and brought into communion with God. It is not merely external information or a neutral opportunity. Prayer, sacrament, ascetic struggle, and virtue are modes of a grace-sustained life.

## Conversion

Synergy names cooperation between divine grace and human response. God initiates, sustains, and brings to completion; the human person's consent and participation are real. The model is better imagined as healed participation than as two causes supplying percentages.

## Election and predestination

Orthodox theology affirms foreknowledge and providence while commonly rejecting unconditional reprobation or a decree that makes sin necessary. John of Damascus distinguishes divine foreknowledge from predetermination of matters “in our power.” Modern Orthodox authors do not all formulate election in the same way.

## Perseverance and apostasy

A believer must persevere in repentant communion; apostasy is possible. Confidence rests in God's faithfulness and mercy rather than a doctrine that every regenerate person must infallibly persevere.

## Formative theologians

The tradition has no single founder. Key patristic sources include Athanasius, the Cappadocians, John Chrysostom, [Maximus the Confessor](../church-fathers/greek-fathers.md#maximus-the-confessor), and [John of Damascus](../church-fathers/greek-fathers.md#john-of-damascus). Gregory Palamas is important for later accounts of participation in divine energies.

Maximus's distinction between natural and gnomic willing is Christological and ascetic, not a restatement of libertarianism. See the [glossary](../glossary.md#natural-will).

## Internal diversity and caution

Jurisdictions share liturgy, councils, and patristic inheritance, not a single modern systematic manual on free will. Representative modern writers may illuminate the tradition but should not be treated as speaking alone for all Orthodoxy. This page therefore avoids claims about a uniquely “official Orthodox theory” of causal determination.

## Reformed Assessment

Orthodoxy's strongest contribution is its relational and participatory account of grace, healed freedom, sacrament, and theosis. From a confessional Reformed perspective, synergy leaves the final differentiating response less fully explained, its anthropology gives less weight to Augustinian-Reformed moral inability, and unconditional election and effectual calling carry less causal force. This assessment does not identify Orthodoxy with Pelagianism. See [Why the Orthodox Synergistic Account Is Weaker](../reformed-evaluation.md#why-the-orthodox-synergistic-account-is-weaker) and the [seventeen-dimension assessment](../reformed-dimensions-biblical-case.md).
