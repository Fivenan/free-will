# Reformed / Presbyterian

## Summary

Confessional Reformed theology teaches voluntary creaturely action under comprehensive providence, total inability in saving matters, unconditional election, effectual calling, justification by faith, and God's preservation of the saints. Later Reformed accounts are commonly compatibilist, though the confessions do not endorse one modern analytic theory.

## Human nature and the Fall

The will retains “natural liberty” and is not forced by an absolute necessity of nature. After the Fall, however, a person cannot convert or prepare themselves for spiritual good accompanying salvation. This is inability of disposition and spiritual condition, not absence of deliberation.

## Grace and conversion

The outward gospel call addresses many and can be rejected. In effectual calling God enlightens the mind, renews the will, and draws the elect so that they come willingly. Regeneration is commonly treated as logically prior to faith; faith is the response produced by renewing grace.

## Election and predestination

Election is unconditional and not grounded in foreseen faith. Reprobation is usually formulated asymmetrically: Westminster speaks of passing by and ordaining to judgment for sin, while Reformed theologians differ on supra-/infralapsarian ordering and the mode of divine causality. This is not a symmetrical claim that God produces faith and unbelief in the same moral way.

## Perseverance

Those truly united to Christ are preserved and persevere. They can fall into serious sin and lose assurance for a time, but not totally and finally lose saving grace. The Canons of Dort ground perseverance in divine mercy and use of means.

## Free-will dimensions

| Dimension | Confessional Reformed emphasis |
|---|---|
| Alternatives | Not normally required for responsibility |
| Sourcehood/voluntariness | Strong; providence does not make creatures coerced |
| Fallen saving ability | None by natural strength |
| Grace | Effectual for the elect |
| Conversion | Monergistic |
| Election | Unconditional |
| Apostasy | Not final for the truly regenerate |
| Modern comparison | Commonly compatibilist |

## Formative theologians and diversity

[Calvin](../tradition-builders/calvin.md), Theodore Beza, Francis Turretin, and [Jonathan Edwards](../tradition-builders/calvin.md#jonathan-edwards) are important but not interchangeable. Reformed Baptist, Presbyterian, Continental Reformed, and Anglican Reformed traditions share family resemblances while differing on covenants, church, and sacraments.

## Common misunderstandings

- Reformed theology does not normally say people are robots; it insists that choices are voluntary and morally significant.
- “Irresistible grace” names successful renewal of the will, not belief by external compulsion.
- Predestination is not fatalism: preaching, deliberation, warning, and faith are ordained means.

## Reformed Self-Assessment

This self-assessment states the confessional case rather than presenting it as neutral description.

### Principal strengths

- comprehensive providence with real secondary causes;
- decisive grace and monergistic regeneration;
- unconditional election and effectual calling;
- preservation and perseverance;
- compatibilist voluntary responsibility.

### Principal objections requiring defense

- the author-of-sin problem and the wider problem of evil;
- moral responsibility under determination;
- universal-saving-will passages;
- ultimate-sourcehood, manipulation, and transformed-desire objections.

The dedicated [Reformed evaluation](../reformed-evaluation.md) presents the cumulative case, opposing readings, comparative costs, and these objections in full.
