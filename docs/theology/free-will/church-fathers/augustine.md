# Augustine on Free Will and Grace

Augustine of Hippo (354–430) is a turning point because he develops both an influential defense of voluntary responsibility and an increasingly strong doctrine of original sin, efficacious grace, predestination, and the gift of perseverance.

> **Disputed classification**
>
> Later Augustine contains important ideas analogous to later compatibilist accounts, especially willing action shaped by transformed desire. Calling him simply “a compatibilist,” however, projects a modern debate onto changing ancient arguments.

## Anti-Manichaean context

Manichaeism explained evil through opposed cosmic principles and treated the self in ways Augustine came to regard as evasive of responsibility. After leaving the movement, he argues that evil is not a substance created by God: moral evil is a defect of will. Divine foreknowledge does not compel the act God knows; God knows voluntary acts as voluntary.

## Early Augustine

In *On Free Choice of the Will* (*De libero arbitrio*, begun in the 380s and completed in the 390s), Augustine connects law, praise, blame, and just punishment with free choice. The will's misuse, not the good Creator, explains sin. Books II and III broaden the account to providence, foreknowledge, order, and the will's fall.

“Early” should not mean “graceless.” Augustine already treats truth and beatitude as divine gifts. The later shift concerns the depth of inherited disorder and the kind of grace needed to begin and persevere in saving willing.

## From *Confessions* to *To Simplicianus*

*Confessions* VIII depicts a divided will: the person wills and does not will, weakened by habit. The problem is neither an external substance nor simple physical constraint. Around 396, Augustine's rereading of Romans in *To Simplicianus* moves election and the effective call to the center. Faith cannot function as an antecedent merit that explains election.

## The Pelagian controversy

The controversy sharpened several claims:

- Adam's sin damages descendants rather than serving only as a bad example.
- Infant baptism bears on inherited sin and the universality of grace.
- Law and instruction reveal but do not by themselves heal the will.
- The grace of Christ is interior and effective, not merely creation, forgiveness, or external teaching.
- Even the beginning of faith cannot be credited to unaided human initiative.

Pelagius, Caelestius, and Julian of Aeclanum should not be treated as one undifferentiated position. Augustine's own formulations also develop across the controversy.

## Later Augustine

### Bondage and real willing

Fallen people retain will and act voluntarily; they do not become non-rational objects. Their problem is a disordered love and inability to achieve saving righteousness without grace. Grace does not merely add an option to an otherwise neutral will: it heals, delights, and produces a good will.

### Efficacious grace

In *On Grace and Free Will*, Augustine refuses both denial of choice and denial of grace. Commands address a will, while the good willing by which commands are fulfilled is itself God's gift. *On Rebuke and Grace* describes grace as giving both the ability and the persevering will, yet still treats rebuke and exhortation as means God uses.

### Predestination and perseverance

In *On the Predestination of the Saints*, the beginning of faith belongs to God's gift and election is not based on foreseen merit. *On the Gift of Perseverance* distinguishes temporary faith from the further divine gift of persevering to the end. Augustine admits that the distribution of this gift is inscrutable.

### Transformed desire

The later view is not best summarized as coercion. The will comes willingly because grace changes what it loves and supplies delight in the good. This feature later supports compatibilist-like readings. Yet Augustine also speaks of self-determination, divine assistance, and freedom as liberation for righteousness, so modern classifications remain contested.

## Is Augustine a compatibilist?

A careful answer separates comparisons:

| Question | Augustine's mature emphasis | Modern comparison |
|---|---|---|
| Does foreknowledge compel choice? | No; God foreknows willing as willing | Compatible with several free-will theories |
| Does fallen desire constrain saving action? | Yes; the will needs liberation by grace | Moral-inability theme |
| Does efficacious grace eliminate willing? | No; it produces a good and willing response | Compatibilist-like transformed-desire account |
| Are alternative possibilities central to saving conversion? | Less central than grace, love, and liberation | Unlike many libertarian accounts |
| Is Augustine using causal determinism as modern philosophy defines it? | Not as his organizing framework | Direct classification is anachronistic |

The [SEP Augustine entry](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/augustine/) documents both the development and interpretive controversy.

## Augustine's legacy

- **Medieval Latin theology:** grace, merit, predestination, and freedom are debated through Augustinian texts.
- **Aquinas:** integrates Augustine with Aristotelian action theory and divine/secondary causation.
- **Luther:** appeals to Augustine while radicalizing the polemic against free choice in saving matters.
- **Calvin and Reformed theology:** receive Augustine on election, grace, and perseverance through new confessional systems.
- **Catholic grace controversies:** Thomist/Bañezian, Molinist, and later Jansenist disputes all claim or contest Augustinian inheritances.
- **Modern philosophy:** Augustine is studied as a major theorist of will, action, evil, and divided agency.

Influence is not identity: Augustine is not “Calvin before Calvin,” and neither Aquinas nor Luther simply repeats him.
