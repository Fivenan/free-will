# Thomas Aquinas

> Thomas Aquinas is a formative medieval Latin theologian whose account of intellect, will, providence, secondary causation, grace, and predestination anchors Thomist debates. He is neither the founder of Catholicism nor easily classed as a modern libertarian or compatibilist.

## Historical context

**Dates:** c. 1225–1274  
**Tradition:** Dominican, Latin Catholic, Scholastic  
**Context:** Aristotelian reception, university theology, mendicant life

## Major works

*Summa theologiae*; *Summa contra Gentiles*; *Disputed Questions on Truth*; *Disputed Questions on Evil*; commentaries on Aristotle and Scripture.

## Anthropology and free will

The intellect presents an apprehended good; the will is a rational appetite ordered toward universal good. Because particular goods do not exhaust that object, the will is not necessitated by any finite good simply as such. Deliberation and practical judgment make a person master of acts and responsible.

Aquinas also holds that God is the universal first cause who moves every created cause. Divine causation does not replace a creature's mode of action: necessary causes act necessarily and contingent voluntary causes act contingently and voluntarily. Secondary causation is real because the first cause establishes and sustains it.

## Grace

Grace is needed not only to heal sin but to elevate humanity to the supernatural end of beatitude. In justification God moves the adult will to assent and repent; because God moves a rational creature according to its nature, the movement of free choice belongs within, not outside, the effect of grace.

## Predestination and election

Predestination is part of providence and its cause is divine goodness rather than antecedent human merit. Aquinas nevertheless insists that prayer, merit as enabled by grace, and secondary causes are real means within providence. His account of reprobation distinguishes permission of defect from divine causation of sin.

## Influence and later schools

Later Thomists, especially [Domingo Báñez](molina.md#domingo-banez), emphasize intrinsically efficacious premotion. Molinists challenge that explanation while claiming fidelity to Aquinas. Contemporary analytic interpreters likewise divide over whether Aquinas's contingency and divine motion best resemble source compatibilism, libertarianism, or a framework that cuts across both.

## Common misunderstandings

- Aquinas does not make creatures inert occasions of divine action.
- He does not teach that unaided will earns the first grace.
- His affirmation of predestination does not make sin positively caused by God.
- “Aquinas was a compatibilist/libertarian” needs an argument and qualification.
