# Jacobus Arminius

> Jacobus Arminius gives grace—not autonomous natural choice—the decisive priority. His dispute concerns the universality and resistibility of grace, conditional election, and providence within the Dutch Reformed context.

## Historical context

**Dates:** 1560–1609  
**Tradition:** Dutch Reformed theologian; formative antecedent of Remonstrant and evangelical Arminian traditions  
**Context:** Leiden controversies over predestination, grace, and confessional interpretation

## Major works

*Declaration of Sentiments*; *Public Disputations*; *Private Disputations*; correspondence and apologetic writings. The Five Articles of the Remonstrance were issued by followers in 1610 after his death and should not be treated as his own text.

## Anthropology and free will

Arminius denies that fallen free choice can begin or perfect any true spiritual good without grace. Mind, affections, and will all require renewal. He calls grace absolutely necessary and says it goes before, accompanies, and follows the good act.

His account nevertheless preserves the possibility of resisting grace. A grace-enabled will can assent or refuse; this makes his orientation libertarian-leaning in saving response, though his scholastic account of concurrence is more detailed than a slogan about “choice.”

## Grace and salvation

Christ's grace illuminates, inclines, assists, and sustains. Justification is by faith and excludes meritorious self-reliance. Arminian synergy therefore begins only with grace already active.

> **Arminianism does not mean that fallen humans can save themselves by autonomous natural free will.**

## Predestination and foreknowledge

Arminius rejects an unconditional decree that logically places particular election and reprobation before the Fall and the provision of Christ. He presents election in Christ of believers, with foreknowledge of faith and perseverance. Providence remains meticulous, but God does not necessitate culpable acts in a way that destroys their contingency.

## Perseverance

Arminius treats final apostasy cautiously and says the question requires further scriptural inquiry. Later Remonstrants affirm its possibility more clearly. It is therefore inaccurate to assign every later Arminian conclusion to Arminius without qualification.

## Position across the dimensions

| Dimension | Arminius |
|---|---|
| Alternatives | Strong grace-enabled emphasis |
| Fallen ability | None for true spiritual good without grace |
| Grace | Preceding, accompanying, sustaining |
| Grace resistible | Yes |
| Foreknowledge | Compatible with contingent acts |
| Election | Conditional/Christ-centered |
| Apostasy | Cautious/underdeveloped in his own work |
| Modern comparison | Libertarian-leaning |

## Influence

The Remonstrants formulate five contested articles; the Synod of Dort answers them. Later evangelical Arminianism, Wesley, Methodism, Holiness churches, many General Baptists, and many Pentecostals receive and transform Arminian themes.

## Common misunderstandings

- Arminius does not teach Pelagian natural ability.
- His theology is not identical to every later Remonstrant position.
- Conditional election does not mean election is merited.
- Resistibility is a claim about grace-enabled agency, not grace's absence.

## Reformed Assessment

Arminius's strongest contribution is his unambiguous insistence that every saving movement depends on preceding, accompanying, and sustaining grace; Arminianism is not Pelagianism. From a confessional Reformed perspective, resistible universal enablement leaves the decisive difference between belief and resistance in the creaturely response, makes election responsive to foreknown faith, and commonly makes final salvation conditional on continued non-resistance.
