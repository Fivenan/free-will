Confessional Reformed Evaluation: Seventeen Dimensions

Confessional evaluation: This page argues from the Westminster Standards and the Canons of Dort. It represents a Reformed judgment, not the wiki's neutral editorial voice. Alternative readings are stated in their strongest plausible form before evaluation.

The descriptive definitions, biblical dossiers, and complete tradition matrix should be read alongside this assessment. Scripture references are linked for contextual reading. The argument is cumulative: no isolated proof text bears the whole system.

Interpretive framework

The Reformed model makes five distinctions:

  • natural agency / moral ability: possessing rational and voluntary powers is not the same as desiring God savingly;
  • determination / coercion: a certain act can still proceed willingly from the agent's own reasons and desires;
  • certainty / kinds of necessity: certainty in God's decree is not physical compulsion or an “absolute necessity of nature”;
  • primary / secondary causation: God governs events as Creator while creatures act as morally differentiated causes;
  • monergism / inactivity: God alone raises the spiritually dead, then the renewed person actively believes, repents, and obeys.

These distinctions need biblical warrant and cannot function as verbal escape hatches. Each section therefore identifies its hardest pressure point.

Agency

1. Alternative possibilities

Reformed answer: Responsibility does not always require that, with the complete past and divine decree fixed, an agent could choose the contrary. Conditional powers—what a person could do if they willed otherwise—and responsiveness to reasons remain real.

Biblical case: Scripture issues genuine alternatives in Deuteronomy 30:15–20, Joshua 24:14–24, and 1 Corinthians 10:13, yet also joins fixed divine purpose to blameworthy action in Acts 2:22–24; 4:27–28. The Reformed case concludes that commands and live deliberation need not entail categorical power to falsify God's plan.

Strongest alternative and its texts: Libertarian accounts argue that “choose,” divine lament, and offered escape normally imply a genuinely open contrary; otherwise moral address is misleading.

Hinge and assessment: The hinge is whether biblical responsibility ever survives a settled outcome. The crucifixion and Matthew 26:24 strongly suggest that it does, so the Reformed account is judged stronger than a universal principle of alternate possibilities.

Hardest objection / response / remainder: The consequence argument says no one controls God's decree or its consequences. Reformed theology answers that control required for responsibility is guidance through one's own rational-voluntary mechanism, not control of the ultimate past. Why that control is sufficient remains a genuine philosophical dispute.

2. Sourcehood

Reformed answer: A person is the proximate, morally significant source of action when it flows from that person's heart, intentions, and reasons, though God is the ultimate Creator and governor.

Biblical case: Evil proceeds from within in Mark 7:20–23; temptation is traced to one's desire rather than God in James 1:13–15; and Joseph's brothers and God intend the same event differently in Genesis 50:20. Scripture assigns ownership by the creature's motive without making creaturely sourcehood ultimate.

Strongest alternative and its texts: Ultimate-source incompatibilism argues that a responsible agent must be the originating source of the character and choice. Appeals to heart and intention seem hollow if God decisively determines both.

Hinge and assessment: Scripture consistently grounds blame in what agents love and intend, not in self-creation. The Reformed layered account therefore better fits the biblical distribution of intentions.

Hardest objection / response / remainder: The manipulation objection compares divine formation of desire to covert programming. The response is that Creator-creature providence, wise governance through natures, and moral corruption are unlike one creature bypassing another's agency. The analogy still exposes the need to explain why ultimate determination does not transfer culpability to God.

3. Voluntariness

Reformed answer: Freedom relevant to ordinary responsibility is action without external compulsion according to one's own understanding and desire. Effectual grace does not drag an unwilling person; it renews the will.

Biblical case: Psalm 110:3 associates divine power with willing response; Philemon 14 and 2 Corinthians 9:7 distinguish willing acts from compulsion; John 6:37 joins the Father's gift to actual coming.

Strongest alternative and its texts: Incompatibilists answer that noncoercion is too thin: a desire implanted determinatively may be “one's own” only formally. Biblical invitations appear to address agents who can resist even inward grace.

Hinge and assessment: The question is whether renewal perfects personal agency or replaces it. Heart-renewal promises in Ezekiel 36:26–27 portray transformed willing, favoring the Reformed account.

Hardest objection / response / remainder: Transformed-desire cases ask whether love produced infallibly is authentic. Reformed theology answers that holiness restores the will to its created end; authenticity does not require self-causing one's character. The phenomenology of ownership after decisive change remains difficult.

4. Reasons-responsiveness

Reformed answer: Human beings can recognize and act for reasons in domains appropriate to their character. Fallen inability concerns saving love of God, not the disappearance of rational responsiveness.

Biblical case: God reasons and warns in Isaiah 1:16–20; Jesus' hearers are answerable for evidence in Matthew 11:20–24; Paul persuades in Acts 17:2–4. Philippians 2:12–13 places human working within divine working.

Strongest alternative and its texts: Libertarians argue that reasons are genuine only if they incline without determining, leaving selection among live alternatives to the agent.

Hinge and assessment: Biblical exhortation assumes that reasons are ordained means, not independent causal exceptions. Reformed theology better unifies divine action and meaningful persuasion.

Hardest objection / response / remainder: If responses are settled, deliberation can seem epiphenomenal. The reply is that decreed ends occur through deliberative means. This explains efficacy, though it does not give agents ultimate control over which reasons prevail.

5. Moral responsibility

Reformed answer: Responsibility rests on God's righteous norm, knowledge, voluntary heart-action, and fitting accountability—not on unaided saving capacity or ultimate self-origination.

Biblical case: Ezekiel 18:20–32 affirms personal accountability; Luke 12:47–48 grades responsibility by knowledge; Romans 1:18–32 traces guilt to suppression and disordered love. Acts 2:23 simultaneously affirms a definite plan and blame.

Strongest alternative and its texts: The ability principle—“ought implies can”—appeals to commands, invitations, and divine justice. Responsibility seems unfair when a person cannot avoid the sinful condition from which acts flow.

Hinge and assessment: Scripture distinguishes created duty from fallen ability and treats self-incurred bondage as culpable. The Reformed reading better preserves both inability texts and universal accountability.

Hardest objection / response / remainder: Individuals did not personally choose Adam's fall. The Reformed appeal to covenant representation, solidarity, and each person's willing ratification of sin answers part of the concern, but inherited corruption and guilt remain among the model's hardest moral questions.

Determination and knowledge

6. Causal determination

Reformed answer: Scripture does not teach modern physical determinism. It teaches that every event is certain within divine providence and occurs through created causes according to their kinds.

Biblical case: Lots, plans, and royal hearts remain genuine secondary causes under God's government in Proverbs 16:1, 9, 33; 21:1. Isaiah 10:5–15 assigns Assyria an intention distinct from God's while both act.

Strongest alternative and its texts: Libertarian concurrence, Molinism, and many Catholic or Orthodox models affirm providence without universal causal determination. Special cases need not yield a universal rule.

Hinge and assessment: The Reformed claim depends on the breadth of Ephesians 1:11 and the cumulative pattern detailed in the sovereignty dossier. It is stronger as a doctrine of providential certainty than as a modern theory of event causation.

Hardest objection / response / remainder: The consequence argument returns here. Secondary causation explains how acts remain creaturely, but critics deny that derivative sourcehood supplies control. Scripture's own concurrent attributions support the Reformed answer without ending the analytic debate.

7. Divine determination

Reformed answer: God's eternal, wise, and holy decree encompasses all that occurs, yet God is not the author of sin and does no violence to creaturely agency.

Biblical case: Genesis 45:5–8; 50:20, Isaiah 46:9–11, Daniel 4:34–35, and Acts 4:27–28 describe effective purpose reaching good and evil events. Different intentions prevent moral equivalence.

Strongest alternative and its texts: Permission or middle-knowledge accounts preserve definite foreknowledge and governance while denying that God determines evil choices. Jeremiah 19:5 and James 1:13 strongly distance evil from God's willing.

Hinge and assessment: Whether “permission” is itself governed within a comprehensive purpose, and whether concurrent texts require more than foreknown use, are decisive. The Reformed account best fits the same-event/different-intentions pattern.

Hardest objection / response / remainder: The author-of-sin and problem-of-evil objections are severe. Reformed theology distinguishes ordaining from committing, holy purpose from evil motive, and asymmetric causation of good and evil. It can affirm mystery only after those distinctions, but why this world and these evils were ordained remains unresolved to creatures.

8. Divine foreknowledge

Reformed answer: God knows all things because he perfectly knows himself, his decree, and all creaturely history; foreknowledge of persons often carries relational and purposive force.

Biblical case: Psalm 139:1–16, Isaiah 46:9–11, Acts 2:23, and Romans 8:29–30 connect exhaustive knowledge with purpose and fulfillment.

Strongest alternative and its texts: Classical non-Reformed views affirm exhaustive foreknowledge without determinative decree; Molinism adds knowledge of libertarian counterfactuals. Open theism reads some future free acts as not yet definite, appealing to divine regret and conditional prophecy such as Jeremiah 18:7–10.

Hinge and assessment: Whether future contingents possess truth independent of decree and whether biblical “foreknow” means foresee or fore-love. The purposive sequence in Romans 8 favors the Reformed reading over election by bare foresight.

Hardest objection / response / remainder: Critics allege modal collapse: if God's necessary nature entails one decree, no other world is possible. Reformed theology denies that God's free decree follows by natural necessity and distinguishes necessity of the consequence from necessity of the consequent. Explaining divine freedom without unrealized potential in God remains a deep metaphysical task.

Sin, grace, and salvation

9. Spiritual ability

Reformed answer: Fallen people retain natural faculties but lack moral ability to turn to God savingly until renewed.

Biblical case: John 6:44, 65; 8:34–36, Romans 8:7–8, 1 Corinthians 2:14, and Ephesians 2:1–5 use inability, slavery, non-reception, and death. The inability dossier shows the cumulative case.

Strongest alternative and its texts: Catholic, Orthodox, Lutheran, Arminian, and Wesleyan positions all require prior grace but differ over the Fall and enabling. Commands to repent and believe are taken to presuppose grace-supplied capacity in their hearers.

Hinge and assessment: Scripture clearly denies unaided saving ability; the further question is whether universal enabling is taught. Because no text clearly states an equal restoration of libertarian capacity to all, effectual renewal is judged stronger.

Hardest objection / response / remainder: Responsibility under inability appears unjust. Reformed theology answers that inability is moral—people act from desires they own—and stems from culpable solidarity and sin. The inherited dimension of bondage remains difficult.

10. Grace priority

Reformed answer: Grace is prior not only as invitation and assistance but as the source of the first saving disposition and response.

Biblical case: God opens Lydia's heart in Acts 16:14; grace makes the decisive distinction in 1 Corinthians 4:7; life and faith belong to God's gift in Ephesians 2:1–10 and Philippians 1:29.

Strongest alternative and its texts: Synergistic traditions also affirm priority: grace heals, awakens, and enables a response that it does not determine. Acts 7:51 and the biblical summons to receive grace support resistibility.

Hinge and assessment: Both sides affirm “grace first”; the question is what makes one equally enabled person believe. Reformed efficacy gives the more grace-centered account of the decisive difference.

Hardest objection / response / remainder: Unequal grace seems arbitrary. Reformed theology locates election in wise mercy, never human merit, and insists no sinner is owed renewal. That answers justice more readily than the hidden reason for unequal mercy.

11. Grace efficacy

Reformed answer: Effectual grace successfully renews those God calls; it causes a willing response rather than bypassing the will.

Biblical case: Ezekiel 36:25–27 promises a new heart that walks in God's ways; all the Father's given ones come in John 6:37–45; creation-light imagery explains conversion in 2 Corinthians 4:6.

Strongest alternative and its texts: Sufficient or prevenient grace genuinely enables everyone, while efficacy is completed through free assent. Universal invitations and resistance texts guard against selective inward causation.

Hinge and assessment: Does drawing explain certain coming or only opportunity? John 6's coordination of giving, coming, raising, and losing none favors successful renewal.

Hardest objection / response / remainder: If grace could have been given to all, selective efficacy intensifies the problem of evil and divine love. The response appeals to mercy's freedom and a redemptive purpose displaying justice and grace; the full reason remains unrevealed.

12. Resistibility

Reformed answer: People resist commands, prophets, common operations, and the outward gospel call. Regenerating grace overcomes resistance by changing the heart.

Biblical case: Acts 7:51; 13:46 establishes real outward resistance, while Romans 8:30 describes a call that reaches justification and John 6:37 a gift that reaches coming. See the resistibility dossier.

Strongest alternative and its texts: Matthew 23:37, Luke 7:30, and Acts 7:51 appear to show God's gracious intention frustrated by human refusal.

Hinge and assessment: The Reformed distinction between general and effectual call has textual support in the different outcomes attached to “call.” It is judged stronger if Romans 8's call is causal rather than merely descriptive.

Hardest objection / response / remainder: Critics see two calls as an ad hoc device that weakens the universal invitation. Reformed theology answers that the outward offer is sincere because Christ is truly offered to every hearer, although only renewing grace produces reception. How distinct divine intentions cohere remains contested.

13. Regeneration and faith order

Reformed answer: Regeneration is logically prior to saving faith; temporally they may be inseparable. Faith is the renewed person's immediate, active reception of Christ.

Biblical case: Divine begetting is not traced to human willing in John 1:12–13; new birth enables perception in John 3:3–8; making alive precedes the description of faith as gift in Ephesians 2:4–10; 1 John 5:1 relates believing to having been born of God.

Strongest alternative and its texts: John 1:12, Acts 2:38, and repeated “believe and live” formulas make faith the condition for new life. Grace enables this faith without regenerating first.

Hinge and assessment: Causal and logical order should not be read directly off sentence order. The inability and new-creation motifs favor regeneration as source, while gospel grammar rightly requires that no one be pronounced saved apart from faith.

Hardest objection / response / remainder: Prior regeneration can sound like salvation before union with Christ by faith. Reformed accounts answer that effectual calling, regeneration, faith, and union form one complex application with logical distinctions, not a saved interval of unbelief. Precise ordering varies within the tradition.

14. Monergism and synergism

Reformed answer: The Spirit alone causes regeneration. The regenerated person—not God instead of the person—believes, repents, and cooperates actively in sanctification.

Biblical case: Birth depends on God in John 1:13; 3:8, resurrection life is given in Ephesians 2:4–5, and active obedience rests on divine operation in Philippians 2:12–13.

Strongest alternative and its texts: Catholic, Orthodox, Arminian, and Wesleyan synergy treats the enabled “yes” as a real, nonmeritorious response. 2 Corinthians 6:1 speaks of working together and not receiving grace in vain.

Hinge and assessment: The relevant moment must be specified. Biblical cooperation in Christian life does not prove cooperation in the production of new birth. Reformed monergism best fits the birth, resurrection, and creation metaphors.

Hardest objection / response / remainder: Monergism may appear to make evangelism, sacraments, and faith idle. The answer is that God ordains these as effectual means and faith as the indispensable human act. The account must keep that act personally meaningful, not merely evidential.

15. Election

Reformed answer: God unconditionally chooses particular sinners in Christ unto salvation; faith and holiness are appointed results, not foreseen grounds.

Biblical case: Romans 8:28–30; 9:6–24, Ephesians 1:3–14, 2 Thessalonians 2:13–14, and 2 Timothy 1:9 ground salvation in purpose and grace before works. See the election dossier and Romans 9 treatment.

Strongest alternative and its texts: Conditional and corporate accounts place election in Christ and condition individual participation on grace-enabled faith. Universal-saving-will texts and Paul's emphasis on faith in Romans 9:30–10:21 support this reading.

Hinge and assessment: Does election explain faith, or ratify foreseen faith? The golden chain and Ephesians' purpose language favor election as source; corporate dimensions do not exclude elected individuals.

Hardest objection / response / remainder: Unconditional election appears partial and incompatible with God's universal love. The response distinguishes justice owed to none from mercy freely given and reads universal texts as sincere invitation or all kinds. The emotional and pastoral force of the objection should not be minimized.

16. Reprobation

Reformed answer: Reprobation is asymmetric with election. God passes over some and ordains their just condemnation for sin; he does not create unbelief in the same gracious manner by which he creates faith.

Biblical case: Hardening and mercy stand together in Romans 9:17–24, judicial hardening appears in Romans 11:7–10, and 1 Peter 2:7–8 joins stumbling and appointment. Condemnation is repeatedly “for sin.”

Strongest alternative and its texts: Lutheranism affirms election to salvation but refuses a parallel decree; Catholicism denies predestination to hell; Orthodox, Wesleyan, and Arminian accounts typically ground final condemnation in foreknown or freely persisted unbelief. Ezekiel 33:11, 1 Timothy 2:4, and 2 Peter 3:9 supply their strongest pressure.

Hinge and assessment: The issue is not whether God causes sin as sin, which Reformed confessions deny, but whether passing over belongs to a prior saving purpose. Romans 9's mercy/hardening contrast gives the Reformed position substantial warrant, though differing orders of the decrees should not be flattened.

Hardest objection / response / remainder: Unequal grace and the “double decree” objection press divine goodness. Asymmetry, universal guilt, and punishment for actual sin answer the charge of equal causation; they do not disclose why mercy was not extended to all.

17. Perseverance

Reformed answer: God preserves the truly regenerate through persevering faith, warnings, discipline, church, and sacraments. Serious falls occur, but not total and final loss of union with Christ.

Biblical case: John 6:37–40; 10:27–30, Romans 8:28–39, Philippians 1:6, 1 Peter 1:3–9, and Jude 24 place final confidence in divine keeping. The perseverance dossier collects the promise case.

Strongest alternative and its texts: Lutheran, Catholic, Orthodox, Wesleyan, many Baptist, and Pentecostal accounts read John 15:1–10, Romans 11:17–22, and Hebrews 6:4–12; 10:26–39 as genuine possibilities of a believer's final apostasy.

Hinge and assessment: Whether profound covenant participation equals irreversible regeneration, and whether promises are individual and unconditional. The tight connection between calling, justification, and glorification in Romans 8 favors Reformed preservation; warning texts remain fully operative means.

Hardest objection / response / remainder: The Reformed reading can make apostasy warnings appear hypothetical or relabel every apostate as never regenerate. The stronger response recognizes real covenant blessings, real danger to every professing hearer, and warnings that effect endurance. Hebrews 6 and 10 remain the hardest cases.

Cumulative judgment

The Reformed model is judged strongest where several lines converge: same-event divine and human intentions, explicit spiritual inability, heart-creating grace, election that issues in faith, and preservation grounded in God's purpose. Its account also has real costs. It must defend responsibility without ultimate self-origination, distinguish ordination from authorship of sin, explain the sincerity of universal summons, and confess limited knowledge about unequal mercy and evil.

That balance is the point of a confessional evaluation: it argues that the Reformed synthesis bears the evidence best while refusing to portray its hardest questions as trivial.
