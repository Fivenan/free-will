Anabaptist / Mennonite

Descriptive: This page maps common Mennonite emphases while preserving major historical Anabaptist diversity and gaps in analytic definition.

Summary

Anabaptism was a diverse sixteenth-century family rather than one theology. Mennonite trajectories emphasize God's saving grace, voluntary faith, new birth, believer's baptism, discipleship, communal discernment, and obedience. These emphases are often synergistic, but must not be equated with Pelagianism.

Human nature and grace

Anabaptist writers oppose inherited church membership and insist on living response to the gospel. This is not necessarily a denial of sin or grace. Contemporary Mennonite confession calls salvation God's gift through Christ and describes human response as yielding to grace, trusting God, repenting, joining the redeemed community, and living the obedience of faith.

Conversion and discipleship

Faith is inseparable from following Jesus, enemy-love, truthfulness, economic sharing, and baptismal allegiance. Regeneration is therefore not merely an invisible causal event; it becomes visible in a Spirit-empowered community.

Election and perseverance

Election and decretal predestination are usually not organizing centers. Many Mennonite accounts allow apostasy and stress enduring discipleship. Historical Anabaptists differ too much for a single formula.

Free-will dimensions

The table maps common Mennonite confessional emphases. Early Swiss, South German, Hutterite, and later Mennonite sources do not supply one analytic system, so “not normally defined” is sometimes the historically accurate answer. See the biblical dossiers.

# Dimension Common Mennonite emphasis Common biblical case
1 Alternative possibilities Voluntary discipleship and refusal are stressed; often libertarian-leaning, not confessionally analytic. Deuteronomy 30:19; Matthew 7:13–27
2 Sourcehood Persons own embodied obedience or disobedience within community; autonomous self-creation is not claimed. Matthew 15:18–20; James 1:14–15
3 Voluntariness Baptism, discipleship, enemy-love, and church commitment must be voluntary. Matthew 16:24; Acts 2:41–47
4 Reasons-responsiveness Jesus' teaching, communal discernment, and visible practice shape responsive moral agency. Matthew 5–7; Romans 12:1–2
5 Moral responsibility Obedience of faith and accountable community life are central; metaphysical prerequisites are rarely systematized. Matthew 25:31–46; James 2:14–26
6 Causal determination Usually not defined confessionally; strong voluntary-response language resists determinist readings. Joshua 24:15; 1 Corinthians 10:13
7 Divine determination Providence is trusted, but decretal determination of sinful choice is not an organizing doctrine. Genesis 50:20; Romans 8:28
8 Divine foreknowledge Classically affirmed; rarely analyzed through a single modern freedom model. Psalm 139:1–16; 1 Peter 1:1–2
9 Spiritual ability Salvation is God's gift; fallen humanity cannot heal or redeem itself. John 15:5; Ephesians 2:1–10
10 Grace priority God's action in Christ and the Spirit precedes yielding, faith, repentance, and baptism. 1 John 4:19; Titus 3:4–7
11 Grace efficacy Grace actually creates and sustains new communal life, while response is not ordinarily called irresistible. 2 Corinthians 5:17; Titus 2:11–14
12 Resistibility Commonly yes; persons and communities can refuse the Spirit and discipleship. Matthew 23:37; Acts 7:51
13 Regeneration / faith order New birth, repentance, faith, baptism, and visible discipleship form an integrated conversion pattern; precise logical order varies. John 3:3–8; Acts 2:38–42
14 Monergism / synergism Commonly synergistic in the sense of grace-enabled yielding and obedience, never an equal causal partnership. 2 Corinthians 6:1; Philippians 2:12–13
15 Election God's people are chosen in Christ for holy communal witness; unconditional individual decree is usually not central. Ephesians 1:3–14; 1 Peter 2:9–12
16 Reprobation Not normally a confessional framework; judgment answers to persistent unbelief and rejected discipleship. Ezekiel 33:11; Matthew 25:41–46
17 Perseverance Enduring discipleship is necessary; apostasy is commonly regarded as possible. Matthew 24:13; Hebrews 3:12–14

Formative figures and diversity

Menno Simons is a major consolidator, not founder of all Anabaptism. Conrad Grebel, Felix Manz, and Balthasar Hubmaier represent different Swiss and South German trajectories; they are not interchangeable. Hubmaier wrote explicitly on freedom of the will, whereas other figures center ecclesiology and discipleship.

Common misunderstandings

  • Anabaptism is not reducible to a generic “free-will Protestantism.”
  • Obedient discipleship is not presented as earning God's initial grace.
  • Modern Mennonite confessions should not be retrojected unchanged onto every sixteenth-century group.
