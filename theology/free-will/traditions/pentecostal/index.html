Pentecostal

Descriptive: This page maps a common classical Pentecostal / Assemblies of God pattern while naming other Pentecostal and charismatic streams.

Summary

Pentecostalism arose through several holiness, revival, and Finished Work networks; it has no single founder and no uniform free-will theory. Many classical Pentecostal bodies inherit Wesleyan or Arminian themes: human inability, universal grace, responsible faith, resistibility, and possible apostasy.

Human nature, grace, and conversion

The Assemblies of God Statement of Fundamental Truths teaches a voluntary Fall producing spiritual death and locates humanity's only hope in Christ. Salvation is received through repentance and faith, with regeneration and renewal attributed to the Holy Spirit and justification to grace.

This official statement does not define agent causation or announce a modern libertarian theory. “Libertarian-leaning” describes a common affinity, not a confessional metaphysic.

Election and perseverance

Classical Pentecostal theology commonly uses conditional-election and conditional-security frameworks. The Assemblies of God explicitly rejects unconditional security and warns that a believer can fall away, while grounding assurance in a living relation with Christ.

Free-will dimensions

This table describes a common classical Pentecostal / Assemblies of God pattern. Holiness-Pentecostal, Finished Work, Oneness, Reformed-charismatic, and global indigenous streams can differ; the analytic labels are approximate. See the biblical dossiers.

# Dimension Common classical Pentecostal emphasis Common biblical case
1 Alternative possibilities Grace-enabled response and refusal are stressed; often libertarian-leaning. Deuteronomy 30:19; Revelation 22:17
2 Sourcehood The hearer owns repentance, faith, and resistance while grace supplies saving possibility. Matthew 15:18–20; Acts 2:37–41
3 Voluntariness Conversion and continuing Spirit-filled obedience are personal, uncoerced responses. Revelation 3:20; 2 Corinthians 3:17
4 Reasons-responsiveness Preaching, testimony, conviction, signs, and Scripture address agents capable by grace of response. John 16:8–11; Acts 2:14–41
5 Moral responsibility Universal summons and enabling support accountability; explicit analytic grounding is uncommon. Acts 17:30–31; Romans 1:18–32
6 Causal determination Commonly rejected for morally free action; not usually an official metaphysical article. Joshua 24:15; 1 Corinthians 10:13
7 Divine determination Strong providence and Spirit-guidance without exhaustive determination of sinful choices. Genesis 50:20; Romans 8:28
8 Divine foreknowledge Classically exhaustive and compatible with enabled free response; not normally analyzed confessionally. Psalm 139:1–16; Romans 8:29
9 Spiritual ability Spiritual death and the Fall exclude independent saving ability; hope rests only in Christ. John 6:44; Ephesians 2:1–5
10 Grace priority The Spirit convicts and grace precedes repentance and faith. John 16:8–11; Titus 2:11
11 Grace efficacy Grace regenerates and transforms those who believe, but does not ordinarily ensure assent. Titus 3:4–7; 2 Corinthians 5:17
12 Resistibility Yes; the Spirit's summons and continuing grace can be refused. Acts 7:51; Hebrews 3:7–15
13 Regeneration / faith order Repentance and faith are the stated conditions of salvation; regeneration is the Spirit's response, with precise logical order seldom defined. Acts 2:38; Titus 3:5
14 Monergism / synergism Usually Arminian/Wesleyan synergy within prior grace; regeneration itself remains the Spirit's work. 2 Corinthians 6:1; Philippians 2:12–13
15 Election Usually conditional and Christ-centered rather than unconditional individual election. Romans 8:29; Ephesians 1:3–14
16 Reprobation Unconditional reprobation is commonly rejected; condemnation follows persistent unbelief. Ezekiel 33:11; 2 Peter 3:9
17 Perseverance Assurance rests in present union with Christ; believers can finally fall away. John 15:1–10; Hebrews 6:4–12

Pioneers and diversity

Charles Fox Parham promoted an early doctrine of tongues as initial evidence and led the Topeka revival; his historical role must be discussed alongside his racism and contested legacy. William J. Seymour's Azusa Street mission became a far more influential interracial and global center. Holiness-Pentecostal, Finished Work, Oneness, Reformed-charismatic, and global indigenous movements differ substantially.

See Pentecostal Pioneers.

Common misunderstandings

  • Pentecostalism was not founded by one man.
  • Experience of the Spirit does not replace a theology of grace or Scripture in classical confessions.
  • Common Arminian affinity does not describe every charismatic or Pentecostal body.

Official and historical sources

  • Assemblies of God, Assurance of Salvation
