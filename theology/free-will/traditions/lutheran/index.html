Lutheran

Descriptive: This page summarizes confessional Lutheran teaching. The section labeled “Reformed Assessment” changes explicitly to a confessional Reformed judgment.

Summary

Confessional Lutheranism distinguishes ordinary civil agency from the fallen person's inability to cause conversion. The Holy Spirit alone creates faith through Word and sacrament; grace is universal and can be resisted, and believers can fall away. This combination differs from both Reformed and Arminian systems.

Human nature and the Fall

The Augsburg Confession and Formula of Concord teach deep original corruption and inability in “spiritual things.” They do not deny reason, voluntary outward action, or civil righteousness. Formula II explicitly says the controversy is not about choices in temporal life.

Grace

The Spirit works through the external Word, baptism, absolution, and the Supper. The gospel is genuinely offered; conversion is not produced by an unaided capacity or a small human contribution. God's action changes an unwilling person into a willing believer.

Conversion

Conversion is monergistic: Scripture attributes its beginning and completion entirely to the Spirit. After conversion, the renewed will cooperates in sanctification, always dependently. Lutheran monergism therefore concerns a defined act, not the denial of all subsequent human action.

Election and predestination

Formula XI teaches eternal election to salvation in Christ as a source of comfort. It directs readers to Word and sacraments rather than a hidden symmetrical decree and denies that God causes sin or wills damnation. Condemnation is traced to sin, unbelief, and resistance.

Perseverance and apostasy

Grace can be resisted and faith can be lost. Lutheran teaching therefore combines monergistic conversion with the possibility of apostasy—evidence that “monergism” does not automatically entail Reformed perseverance.

Free-will dimensions

The table describes confessional Lutheranism rather than every modern Lutheran body. Its combination of monergistic conversion, universal grace, resistibility, and possible apostasy should not be collapsed into either Reformed or Arminian logic. See the biblical dossiers.

# Dimension Confessional Lutheran answer Common biblical case
1 Alternative possibilities Real temporal choices; contrary saving power is denied before conversion. Deuteronomy 30:19; Romans 8:7–8
2 Sourcehood The sinner willingly owns sin; God alone is source of conversion. Matthew 15:19; James 1:13–18
3 Voluntariness Civil action is voluntary; conversion changes an unwilling enemy into a willing believer. Psalm 110:3; Philippians 2:13
4 Reasons-responsiveness Reason functions in temporal affairs; the external Word is the Spirit's saving instrument, not bare information. Romans 10:14–17; Acts 17:2–4
5 Moral responsibility Bondage does not remove guilt; condemnation is for sin and resisted grace. John 3:18–20; Romans 1:18–32
6 Causal determination No confessional modern determinism; saving bondage and divine action are stronger than a general metaphysical theory. John 8:34–36; Acts 17:28
7 Divine determination Providence is strong, but God neither causes sin nor wills damnation by a symmetrical decree. Genesis 50:20; 1 Timothy 2:4
8 Divine foreknowledge Exhaustive; election is not reduced to mere foresight, while foreknowledge does not make God cause evil. Isaiah 46:9–11; Romans 8:29–30
9 Spiritual ability None in spiritual matters by natural powers; ordinary civil agency remains. 1 Corinthians 2:14; Ephesians 2:1–5
10 Grace priority The Spirit through Word and sacrament initiates and completes conversion. John 3:5–8; Romans 10:17
11 Grace efficacy The means of grace truly convey the Spirit's gift; conversion itself is wholly divine action. Isaiah 55:10–11; Titus 3:4–7
12 Resistibility Yes: the offered Word and Spirit can be resisted, without making the person's nonresistance a contribution to conversion. Matthew 23:37; Acts 7:51
13 Regeneration / faith order Baptismal regeneration and faith belong to the Spirit's converting work; Lutheranism resists an abstract interval or causal contribution by faith. John 3:5; Titus 3:5
14 Monergism / synergism Monergistic conversion; the renewed will then cooperates dependently in sanctification. Ephesians 2:4–10; Philippians 2:12–13
15 Election Eternal election to salvation in Christ, proclaimed for comfort; not grounded in merit or a hidden parallel decree. Ephesians 1:3–14; 2 Timothy 1:9
16 Reprobation No symmetrical election to damnation; condemnation is traced to sin, unbelief, and resistance. Ezekiel 33:11; Matthew 23:37
17 Perseverance God supplies preserving grace, yet genuine faith can be lost through repudiation of Word and promise. John 15:1–10; Hebrews 3:12–14

Approximate modern mapping: Saving bondage and monergistic conversion have compatibilist-like features, but universal resistible grace and possible apostasy prevent a simple Reformed-compatibilist classification.

Formative theologians and diversity

Martin Luther frames the Erasmus dispute; Philip Melanchthon shapes the Augsburg Confession and changes some later formulations; Martin Chemnitz is central to confessional consolidation. Confessional Lutheran churches also differ from liberal and other Lutheran bodies in the contemporary authority assigned to the Book of Concord.

Common misunderstandings

  • Luther did not claim that humans lack wills or make no ordinary choices.
  • Lutherans are not “Calvinists with sacraments”: universal grace, means of grace, election, resistance, and apostasy differ.
  • Monergistic conversion does not make preaching and sacrament dispensable; they are the Spirit's instruments.

Reformed Assessment

Confessional Lutheranism is one of the closest alternatives because it strongly preserves bondage of the fallen will, monergistic conversion, and the external means of grace. From a confessional Reformed perspective, the conjunction of monergistic conversion, resistible universal grace, possible apostasy, and an intentionally non-symmetrical account of election leaves a tension that effectual calling and perseverance resolve more systematically. See Why Lutheranism Comes Close but Remains Weaker and the seventeen-dimension assessment.
