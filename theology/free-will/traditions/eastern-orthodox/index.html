Eastern Orthodox

Descriptive: This page summarizes representative Orthodox emphases without imposing a single modern analytic system. The section labeled “Reformed Assessment” changes explicitly to a confessional Reformed judgment.

Summary

Eastern Orthodox theology commonly presents freedom within creation in God's image, ancestral sin, sacramental healing, synergy, and theosis. It strongly affirms divine initiative and genuine response but does not define itself through modern libertarian/compatibilist categories.

Human nature and the Fall

“Ancestral sin” commonly emphasizes mortality, corruption, disordered passions, and solidarity with Adam. Orthodox writers vary in how they discuss inherited culpability and Western “original sin.” Human nature remains God's good creation but needs liberation and healing in Christ.

Grace

Grace is the uncreated divine life and action by which human beings are healed and brought into communion with God. It is not merely external information or a neutral opportunity. Prayer, sacrament, ascetic struggle, and virtue are modes of a grace-sustained life.

Conversion

Synergy names cooperation between divine grace and human response. God initiates, sustains, and brings to completion; the human person's consent and participation are real. The model is better imagined as healed participation than as two causes supplying percentages.

Election and predestination

Orthodox theology affirms foreknowledge and providence while commonly rejecting unconditional reprobation or a decree that makes sin necessary. John of Damascus distinguishes divine foreknowledge from predetermination of matters “in our power.” Modern Orthodox authors do not all formulate election in the same way.

Perseverance and apostasy

A believer must persevere in repentant communion; apostasy is possible. Confidence rests in God's faithfulness and mercy rather than a doctrine that every regenerate person must infallibly persevere.

Free-will dimensions

This is a representative map, not an “official Orthodox analytic theory.” Patristic, Byzantine, and modern Orthodox accounts differ, and several dimensions are not normally framed as Western decree questions. See the biblical dossiers for competing readings.

# Dimension Representative Orthodox emphasis Common biblical case
1 Alternative possibilities Genuine consent and refusal are usually stressed; many accounts are libertarian-leaning without defining a modern thesis. Deuteronomy 30:15–20; Matthew 23:37
2 Sourcehood Persons act from a created nature and hypostatic agency healed toward communion, not autonomous self-origination. Matthew 15:18–20; James 1:14–15
3 Voluntariness Love and theosis require unforced personal participation; ascetic obedience is grace-sustained. Matthew 16:24; 2 Corinthians 3:17–18
4 Reasons-responsiveness The image of God includes rational, purposive agency, though passions darken perception. Romans 12:1–2; Hebrews 5:14
5 Moral responsibility Judgment answers to voluntary sin, knowledge, repentance, and embodied discipleship. Ezekiel 18:20–32; Matthew 25:31–46
6 Causal determination Usually rejected as a universal account of creaturely choice; providence does not eliminate “what is in our power.” Deuteronomy 30:15–20; 1 Corinthians 10:13
7 Divine determination God providentially governs and can redirect history, but sin is not necessitated by a divine decree. Genesis 50:20; Romans 8:28
8 Divine foreknowledge Exhaustive foreknowledge does not itself predetermine voluntary acts. Psalm 139:1–16; 1 Peter 1:1–2
9 Spiritual ability Ancestral sin brings mortality, corruption, and disordered desire; nature cannot heal or deify itself. John 15:5; Romans 5:12–21
10 Grace priority The Triune God's incarnation, call, sacrament, and energy precede and sustain every saving response. John 6:44; 1 John 4:19
11 Grace efficacy Grace really heals and deifies, but its personal fruit is not normally described as an irresistible decree. 2 Peter 1:3–4; 2 Corinthians 3:18
12 Resistibility Persons can grieve the Spirit and refuse divine summons. Isaiah 63:10; Acts 7:51
13 Regeneration / faith order New birth is sacramental and participatory; faith, baptism, Spirit, and incorporation are not usually reduced to one analytic causal sequence. John 3:5; Titus 3:4–7
14 Monergism / synergism Synergy: God initiates and empowers; the human person really consents and participates, without dividing grace into percentages. 1 Corinthians 3:9; Philippians 2:12–13
15 Election Election in Christ and divine foreknowledge are affirmed; unconditional individual decree is not normally the controlling frame. Ephesians 1:3–14; 1 Peter 1:1–2
16 Reprobation No standard symmetrical decree; condemnation is tied to persistent refusal of life and communion. Ezekiel 33:11; Matthew 25:41–46
17 Perseverance Persevering, repentant communion is necessary; final apostasy is possible. John 15:1–10; Hebrews 3:12–14

Formative theologians

The tradition has no single founder. Key patristic sources include Athanasius, the Cappadocians, John Chrysostom, Maximus the Confessor, and John of Damascus. Gregory Palamas is important for later accounts of participation in divine energies.

Maximus's distinction between natural and gnomic willing is Christological and ascetic, not a restatement of libertarianism. See the glossary.

Internal diversity and caution

Jurisdictions share liturgy, councils, and patristic inheritance, not a single modern systematic manual on free will. Representative modern writers may illuminate the tradition but should not be treated as speaking alone for all Orthodoxy. This page therefore avoids claims about a uniquely “official Orthodox theory” of causal determination.

Reformed Assessment

Orthodoxy's strongest contribution is its relational and participatory account of grace, healed freedom, sacrament, and theosis. From a confessional Reformed perspective, synergy leaves the final differentiating response less fully explained, its anthropology gives less weight to Augustinian-Reformed moral inability, and unconditional election and effectual calling carry less causal force. This assessment does not identify Orthodoxy with Pelagianism. See Why the Orthodox Synergistic Account Is Weaker and the seventeen-dimension assessment.
