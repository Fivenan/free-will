Latin Fathers

Latin Christianity developed a particularly influential vocabulary of will, culpability, original sin, grace, and predestination. Tertullian, Augustine, and Cassian illustrate development and disagreement rather than a single “Latin view.”

Tertullian

Tertullian's anti-Marcionite arguments connect law and judgment with voluntary creaturely action. See the fuller early-fathers profile. His work is an antecedent to Latin reflection on voluntas, not a statement of later Augustinian soteriology.

Augustine of Hippo

Dates: 354–430
Region: Roman North Africa
Relevant works: On Free Choice of the Will, Confessions, On the Spirit and the Letter, On Grace and Free Will, On the Predestination of the Saints

Augustine's early anti-Manichaean work defends creaturely free choice as the source of evil. During and after the Pelagian controversy he increasingly argues that the fallen will cannot heal itself, the beginning of faith is a gift, and perseverance depends on efficacious grace. He continues to affirm willing and responsibility; the question becomes how grace liberates the will.

Because this development anchors later Western debates, see the dedicated Augustine page.

John Cassian

Dates: c. 360–c. 435
Region/tradition: probably Scythia; Egyptian monastic formation; founded monasteries near Marseille
Relevant works: Institutes, Conferences, especially Conference 13

Human freedom and grace

Cassian resists both self-sufficient will and an account he thinks makes exhortation idle. Conference 13 assembles biblical cases in which divine initiative is conspicuous and cases in which a human movement seems to be met by grace. Throughout, attainment and perseverance remain gifts of God.

Sin and spiritual ability

His ascetic setting concerns trained, baptized monks as well as conversion. Habit, temptation, prayer, and discipline matter. It is therefore hazardous to turn every claim about ascetic cooperation into a claim about the unaided beginning of faith.

Modern comparison and scholarly caution

Cassian is a major witness to a synergistic Latin trajectory. The label “Semi-Pelagian” was coined later and can obscure his strong doctrines of grace; interpreters disagree over whether Conference 13 assigns any first saving movement to unaided will. State the narrower claim being evaluated rather than using the label as a verdict.

Primary sources

Conference 13; Conferences 1–10; Institutes.

        ↓

        ↓

        ↓
Augustine's predestination and gift of perseverance
        ↘
       Cassian and southern Gallic debates
        ↓

        ↓
medieval Latin syntheses and later Reformation disputes

This diagram is schematic. Cassian was not simply a reaction to a finished “Augustinian system,” and Orange did not settle every later Catholic, Lutheran, Reformed, or Arminian question.
