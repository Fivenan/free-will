Greek Fathers

Greek patristic accounts often connect freedom to creation in God's image, moral and ascetic formation, healing, and participation in divine life. This is a trajectory, not a unanimous school.

Historical caution: “Synergistic” can describe a pattern of divine initiative and human cooperation. It must not be read as the claim that grace and nature are two independent, equal causes.

Athanasius of Alexandria

Dates: c. 296–373
Relevant works: On the Incarnation, Against the Heathen, Life of Antony

Athanasius depicts rational creatures as made for contemplation and communion yet turned toward corruption by misuse. The incarnation restores the image, defeats death, and makes knowledge and holiness possible. His account foregrounds ontological healing more than the later question of whether an isolated decision has alternative possibilities.

Modern comparison: freedom-as-restoration within divine action; anti-necessitarian themes, but no simple analytic label.

Primary sources: On the Incarnation; Against the Heathen.

Basil the Great

Dates: c. 330–379
Relevant works: Hexaemeron, On the Holy Spirit, Longer Rules, homilies

Basil's preaching and ascetic rules presuppose deliberate response, habit, repentance, and accountable action. The Holy Spirit perfects and sanctifies; moral agency is exercised within worship and communal discipline rather than as metaphysical independence.

Modern comparison: synergistic trajectory with strong ascetic and pneumatological context.

Primary sources: On the Holy Spirit; selected homilies.

Gregory of Nazianzus

Dates: 329–390
Relevant works: Theological Orations, moral and festal orations

Gregory holds together divine initiative and a willing human response. His Christology makes the healing of the complete human person central: what is not assumed is not healed. Exhortation and repentance are genuine, yet salvation is gift and transformation.

Modern comparison: synergistic and anti-coercive themes; not a worked-out libertarian theory.

Primary sources: Orations, especially Orations 29–31 and 45.

Gregory of Nyssa

Dates: c. 335–c. 395
Relevant works: On the Making of Man, Great Catechism, Life of Moses

Human freedom and responsibility

Gregory links being made in God's image to rational self-direction and treats evil as a privation arising through mutable choice rather than a divinely created substance. Moral progress and purification presuppose responsive agency.

Grace and spiritual ability

Creation, incarnation, sacrament, and divine attraction frame human movement. Gregory's freedom is teleological: authentic freedom matures toward the Good, not mere indifference among options.

Modern comparison and caution

“Libertarian-leaning” is reasonable for his rejection of compulsion and strong self-determination. Debates over universal restoration, infinity, and the stability of the will prevent a simple placement.

Primary sources

On the Making of Man; Great Catechism; Life of Moses.

John Chrysostom

Dates: c. 347–407
Relevant works: Homilies on Romans, Homilies on John, Homilies on Ephesians

Chrysostom's homiletic method assumes that warnings, commands, repentance, and perseverance address responsible hearers. He repeatedly refuses fatalistic readings of providence and emphasizes willing response. At the same time, he attributes the call, aid, forgiveness, and transformation to God.

Modern comparison: strongly synergistic and anti-fatalist; a modern theory of indeterministic causation should not be inferred from sermon rhetoric alone.

Primary sources: Homilies on Romans; Homilies on John.

Cyril of Alexandria

Dates: c. 376–444
Relevant works: Commentary on John, Against Nestorius, letters

Cyril's decisive context is Christological and sacramental. Union with Christ and the Spirit restores and vivifies humanity. He affirms accountable response, but his treatment should not be reconstructed from isolated vocabulary into a modern free-will model.

Modern comparison: grace-centered participatory agency; classification beyond that is disputed.

Primary sources: letters and conciliar texts; Commentary on John.

Maximus the Confessor

Dates: c. 580–662
Region/tradition: Byzantine monk and theologian
Relevant works: Ambigua, Questions to Thalassius, Disputation with Pyrrhus, Opuscula

Natural and gnomic willing

For Maximus, natural will is the rational appetite or capacity for the good belonging to human nature. Gnomic willing names a mode of deliberative, hesitant self-determination associated with a person who does not clearly know or stably possess the good. It is not simply a second faculty alongside natural will.

Christology

Maximus defended Christ's complete human natural will against Monothelitism. Christ wills humanly and divinely without sinful opposition; attributing gnomic deliberation to Christ would, in Maximus's mature account, introduce ignorance or uncertainty inappropriate to the incarnate Logos. The Sixth Ecumenical Council later confessed two natural wills and operations in Christ.

Grace, freedom, and deification

Human fulfillment is not escape from natural willing but its healing and free conformity to God. Ascetic practice, sacrament, virtue, and grace reorder the person's mode of willing toward the logos of nature. Cooperation belongs within deification: the creature acts because divine life heals and empowers creaturely agency.

Modern comparison

Maximus cannot be flattened into libertarianism versus compatibilism. His account joins nature, hypostasis, deliberation, Christology, and eschatological freedom in categories unlike the modern causal-determinist debate.

Primary sources and scholarship

Disputation with Pyrrhus; Opuscula 3 and 7; Ambigua 7 and 42; Questions to Thalassius 21. For orientation, see Maximus's place in the historical development and the Eastern Orthodox profile. Modern interpretation of gnōmē and its development across Maximus's career remains disputed.

John of Damascus

Dates: c. 675–749
Relevant works: Exact Exposition of the Orthodox Faith

John distinguishes foreknowledge from predetermination: God foreknows what is “in our power” without predetermining it in the same way as events of providence. Deliberation, choice, and action ground responsibility; providence includes willing, permission, and wise governance.

Modern comparison: synergistic and libertarian-leaning themes, but expressed within patristic psychology and providence rather than analytic incompatibilism.

Primary source: Exact Exposition II.22–30.
