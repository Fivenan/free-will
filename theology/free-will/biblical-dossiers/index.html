Biblical Dossiers for Free Will, Grace, and Election

Descriptive and comparative: This page maps the principal biblical arguments used by several Christian traditions. It does not assume that a reference by itself proves a philosophical or dogmatic system.

These dossiers supply the biblical layer for the seventeen dimensions, the tradition profiles, and the explicitly confessional Reformed evaluation. Linked references open a complete passage at Bible Gateway. Readers should examine the literary setting, not only the selected verse.

Four cautions govern the comparisons:

  1. A narrative of divine action is not automatically a theory of causal determinism.
  2. A command proves obligation, but the disputed question is whether it also proves unaided moral ability.
  3. Resistance to a prophet, command, or outward gospel call is not automatically resistance to inward regeneration.
  4. Promise passages and warning passages must be read together; neither set may be made unreal.

1. Divine sovereignty and human responsibility

The cumulative sovereignty case

Biblical pattern Representative passages What the argument claims
God restrains or hardens rulers Genesis 20:6; Exodus 4:21; 7–14; Deuteronomy 2:30; Joshua 11:20; 1 Samuel 2:25 Divine government reaches dispositions and decisions, not only events external to agents. Exodus also ascribes hardening to Pharaoh, so agency is not erased.
One event has divine and human intentions Genesis 45:5–8; 50:20; Isaiah 10:5–15; Acts 2:22–24; 4:27–28 God's good purpose and a creature's evil purpose can concur in the same event without moral symmetry. The crucifixion is the strongest canonical instance.
Plans and apparently contingent means are governed Proverbs 16:1, 4, 9, 33; 19:21; 21:1; Daniel 4:34–35 Providence includes deliberation, royal decisions, and lots while preserving their character as secondary causes.
God declares and accomplishes his purpose Isaiah 46:8–11; Ephesians 1:3–14; Philippians 2:12–13 God's purpose is effective, yet the ordained result includes rather than bypasses human working.

The Reformed reading treats these texts as a cumulative case for comprehensive providence through real secondary causes. It distinguishes certainty—the event will occur in God's plan—from coercion, and divine primary causation from creaturely causation.

The strongest alternative grants meticulous providence in some events but denies that the texts universalize causal determination of every choice. It reads hardening as judicial, sometimes responsive to prior sin; reads the crucifixion as a specially ordained redemptive event; and explains ordinary action through divine permission, concurrence, foreknowledge, or middle knowledge. The hinge is whether the universal language of Ephesians 1:11 and the repeated canonical pattern warrant a general doctrine.

2. Fallen inability

Text clusters

  • Genesis 6:5; 8:21, Jeremiah 13:23; 17:9, and Romans 3:9–20 describe pervasive corruption; the dispute is whether this entails inability or only universal sinfulness.
  • John 3:3–8; 6:44, 65; 8:34–36 uses birth, divine drawing, and slavery. Reformed and Lutheran readings see inability; many synergistic readings see inability answered by universally offered enabling grace.
  • Romans 6:16–23; 8:5–8, 1 Corinthians 2:14, Ephesians 2:1–10, and Colossians 2:13 describe hostility, inability, spiritual non-reception, death, and divine making-alive.

The Reformed case is about moral and spiritual ability, not bodily or rational faculties. Fallen people deliberate and choose voluntarily, yet will not love God savingly because the heart is corrupt. Command therefore establishes duty, not necessarily contrary moral power.

The principal alternatives agree that grace is necessary. Catholic and Orthodox accounts speak of wounded or corrupted nature healed by grace; Lutheranism denies saving ability but affirms universally intended, resistible grace; Arminian and Wesleyan accounts say prevenient grace restores a genuine capacity to respond. The exegetical question is whether Scripture teaches such universal restoration and whether “dead,” “cannot,” and “hostile” name a condition only grace can decisively change.

3. Effectual calling and regeneration

The renewing-action case

Deuteronomy 30:6 and Ezekiel 11:19–20; 36:25–27 promise divine heart-change whose result is willing obedience. John 1:12–13; 3:3–8; 5:21; 6:37–45 joins new birth, divine giving, drawing, and coming. Acts 13:48; 16:14, Romans 8:28–30, 1 Corinthians 1:18–31, and 2 Corinthians 4:6 portray an effective divine summons or opening.

Ephesians 2:1–10, Philippians 1:29, James 1:18, 1 Peter 1:3, 23, and 1 John 5:1 attribute making alive, believing, and birth to God and his word. The Reformed inference is that the inward call creates the willing response it commands; the outward call remains resistible.

Alternatives answer that these texts can describe God's initiative and the believer's new status without fixing a logical order between regeneration and faith. They appeal especially to calls to turn, receive, and believe, such as Deuteronomy 30:11–20, John 1:12, and Acts 2:38. The hinge is whether saving faith is the condition upon which new life is first given or the immediate fruit of God's life-giving summons. Temporal sequence should not be inferred mechanically from logical priority.

4. Election

God's choice of Israel in Deuteronomy 7:6–8 is grounded in love rather than Israel's superiority. Jesus distinguishes divine revelation and human refusal in Matthew 11:20–27; 22:14; 24:22–31, and speaks of the Father's gift in John 6:35–65; 10:22–30; 15:16. Acts 13:48, Romans 8:28–30; 9:6–24; 11:1–10, Ephesians 1:3–14; 2:8–10, 2 Thessalonians 2:13–14, and 2 Timothy 1:8–10 form the principal New Testament case.

The Reformed reading sees individual, saving election grounded in God's gracious purpose and issuing in faith. Conditional-election readings interpret election corporately “in Christ,” or as conditioned on foreknown, grace-enabled faith. Some interpreters combine corporate and individual aspects. The decisive questions are whether foreknowledge means foresight or relational fore-love, whether Romans 9 concerns historical vocation alone or also mercy unto salvation, and whether faith is election's foreseen condition or appointed result.

5. Universal saving will and universal provision

Passage Non-Reformed emphasis Common Reformed response Exegetical hinge
Ezekiel 18:23, 32; 33:11 God takes no pleasure in the wicked person's death and sincerely calls each to turn. The revealed preceptive will can express true compassion without implying a decree to regenerate every hearer. Whether divine willing is univocal across decree, command, disposition, and invitation.
Matthew 23:37 Jesus wills to gather persons who refuse him. Jesus addresses Jerusalem's leaders or the covenant community; the text displays real outward resistance, whatever its exact objects. Who are “you” and “your children,” and what kind of gathering is willed?
John 1:9, 29; 3:16–17; 12:32 “World” and “all” express universal saving provision and enabling. They often deny ethnic restriction and proclaim sufficiency or worldwide scope without teaching equal inward grace. Whether context means every individual or people without distinction.
Romans 5:12–21; 11:32 Christ's saving work corresponds universally to Adam's ruin; mercy is for all. The Adam/Christ parallel concerns two covenant heads and their peoples; Romans 11 moves between Jews and Gentiles. The referent and symmetry of each “all.”
1 Timothy 2:1–6; 4:10 God wants all people saved and Christ ransoms all. The setting stresses all social kinds, including rulers; “Savior” in 4:10 may distinguish providential preservation from saving benefit. Individuals distributively or classes without distinction; what does “especially” mean?
Titus 2:11, Hebrews 2:9, 1 John 2:2 Saving grace and atonement extend to every person. “All” can mean all classes; “everyone” can be bounded by the surrounding “many”; “whole world” can deny local or ethnic limitation. Scope markers in each immediate discourse and the nature of atonement language.
2 Peter 3:9 God wants none whatsoever to perish and delays for universal repentance. “You/any/all” may refer to the beloved covenant audience; the delay ensures all intended recipients repent. The grammatical scope of the pronouns and the relation of patience to election.

These texts are not inconveniences to be dismissed. They establish the sincerity and breadth of gospel proclamation and divine compassion. The debate concerns how they relate to texts about particular election and effective grace.

6. Resistibility

Genesis 6:3, Nehemiah 9:26, 30, Isaiah 63:10, Matthew 23:37, Luke 7:30, Acts 7:51; 13:46, and Romans 10:16–21 plainly portray resistance to God, the Spirit, prophetic ministry, and gospel summons.

Non-Reformed traditions commonly infer that saving grace itself can be finally resisted. Reformed theology accepts the resistance and distinguishes:

  • external or general call: the sincere preached invitation, often rejected;
  • common operations: illumination, restraint, covenant privilege, and conviction that do not regenerate;
  • effectual call: the Spirit's inward renewal that produces willing faith.

The distinction succeeds only if Scripture itself supports two operations, not merely because a system requires them. Reformed readers appeal to the contrast between the called who refuse and the call that brings justification in Matthew 22:14 and Romans 8:30, together with the successful drawing of John 6:37–45. The hinge is whether those texts name an effectual summons or only God's initiative met by a free response.

7. Perseverance and preservation

The promise case includes God's enduring covenant commitment in Jeremiah 32:38–40; passing from death to life in John 5:24; Christ's keeping of those given to him in John 6:37–40; 10:27–30; 17:6–24; and the unbroken saving purpose of Romans 8:28–39.

1 Corinthians 1:4–9, Philippians 1:6, 1 Thessalonians 5:23–24, Hebrews 7:23–25, 1 Peter 1:3–9, 1 John 2:19, and Jude 20–25 ground confidence in God's faithfulness, Christ's intercession, and preservation through faith.

Reformed theology therefore teaches preservation through perseverance, not salvation without continuing faith. Alternatives agree that God is faithful but read many promises as corporate, conditional, or compatible with a believer's later repudiation. Romans 11's warning against presumption and the warning texts below determine how absolute the promises are.

8. Apostasy warnings

Warning cluster Serious apostasy reading Reformed reading
Matthew 24:9–13; John 15:1–10 Disciples genuinely related to Christ can fail to remain and be lost. Visible covenant relation and professed discipleship can be real without final regenerating union; endurance identifies the saved.
Romans 11:17–22; 1 Corinthians 9:24–27; 10:1–12 Believers are warned of a real possibility of final exclusion. Corporate covenant inclusion and warnings are ordained means by which the elect persevere; no hearer may presume.
Galatians 5:1–5; Colossians 1:21–23 Falling from grace and conditional reconciliation name possible loss of saving status. Paul warns professing communities against a path whose embrace would disclose abandonment of the true gospel; the condition describes persevering faith.
Hebrews 3:6–14; 6:4–12; 10:19–39 The subjects share profound saving realities and may finally apostatize. They experience covenant blessings and the Spirit's operations, while the author distinguishes “things that belong to salvation” and expresses confidence about the faithful.
2 Peter 2:1, 18–22 People bought by the Master and escaped from defilement return to ruin. “Bought” can invoke covenant ownership; external escape and knowledge do not necessarily equal regeneration, as the animal proverbs explain.

The Reformed account must not turn warnings into descriptions of impossible worlds. Warnings address the whole church, expose false confidence, summon endurance, and are means of preservation. Conditional-security traditions answer that a warning's natural force includes a genuine possibility that a regenerate believer finally abandons faith. The central hinge is whether covenant participation and profound spiritual experience are identical with irreversible regeneration.

Six disputed passages in focus

Romans 9:6–24

Reformed reading: Paul's examples of Isaac, Jacob, Pharaoh, mercy, hardening, and the potter ground individual saving election and reprobation in God's purpose rather than foreseen willing or running. The objector's responsibility question makes sense because divine determination is strong.

Major alternatives: Corporate-election and salvation-historical readings see Paul defending God's freedom to define the covenant people and assign historical vocations, with Romans 9:30–10:21 emphasizing faith and culpable refusal. Others interpret “vessels” asymmetrically, with the reprobate fitting themselves for destruction.

Hinge: Does Paul's argument stop at corporate vocation, or do “mercy,” “glory,” and the remnant carry individual saving force? How do divine hardening and human unbelief relate? Read Romans 9:6–24 within Romans 9–11.

John 6:35–65

Reformed reading: All whom the Father gives come; the Son loses none; no one can come unless drawn and enabled. Giving and drawing therefore secure rather than merely offer faith.

Major alternatives: Drawing may be universal enabling, especially alongside John 12:32, while coming remains resistible. Some read the Father's “given” people as those already responsive to God under the old covenant.

Hinge: Is the drawing in John 6:35–65 coextensive with resurrection on the last day, and does John 12 define its efficacy or its worldwide scope?

Acts 13:48

Reformed reading: Appointment to eternal life precedes and explains belief.

Major alternatives: The participle can be read as persons disposed or aligned toward eternal life, or appointment can be corporate and mediated through response.

Hinge: The normal force of the passive construction, Luke's use of divine appointment, and the contrast with self-rejection in verse 46. Read Acts 13:44–49.

1 Timothy 2:4

Reformed reading: The prayer context moves across social classes—especially rulers—so “all people” means all kinds without ethnic or social distinction. Alternatively, God's revealed desire can be distinguished from his efficacious decree.

Major alternative: The most natural distributive sense is every person; universal divine desire and provision ground prayer and mission while grace remains resistible.

Hinge: The scope of “all” in 1 Timothy 2:1–6 and whether Scripture warrants distinct senses of divine will.

2 Peter 3:9

Reformed reading: God's patience is directed to “you,” the beloved community; he ensures that all intended recipients reach repentance before the day comes.

Major alternative: “Not wanting anyone to perish” declares God's universal saving will and explains a delay that leaves genuine opportunity to every person.

Hinge: The antecedent and scope of “any/all,” the audience marked in 2 Peter 3:1, 8–9, and the difference between patience and causal efficacy.

Hebrews 6 and 10

Reformed reading: The language describes profound covenant experience—illumination, tasting, sharing in the Spirit's operations—without saying that the persons were regenerate and justified. Hebrews 6:9 distinguishes the better things belonging to salvation; the warnings are effective means of perseverance.

Major alternative: The descriptions most naturally name genuine believers, and the warning loses its force if their final apostasy is impossible. “Sanctified” in Hebrews 10:29 appears especially strong.

Hinge: Whether covenant participation equals inward saving union, who is sanctified in 10:29, and how the severe warnings relate to the author's confidence in Hebrews 6:4–12; 10:19–39.
