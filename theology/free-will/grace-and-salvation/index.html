Grace and Salvation

Christian traditions commonly affirm real human willing, the damage of sin, and the necessity of grace. Their disagreements concern spiritual ability, the mode and efficacy of grace, conversion, election, and perseverance—not whether grace matters.

Ordinary agency is not saving ability. Someone may deliberate, keep civic order, and make voluntary choices while lacking the unaided capacity to initiate saving faith.

Sin and spiritual ability

Original sin in Western traditions and ancestral sin in much Eastern language name humanity's solidarity with Adam and the resulting corruption, mortality, and disorder. They are not interchangeable formulas: traditions differ about inherited guilt, deprivation, and healing. Total inability is the narrower claim that fallen people cannot turn to God savingly by their natural powers; it does not mean inability to perform any socially good act.

Pelagianism

The Pelagian controversy of the early fifth century concerned sin, infant baptism, grace, and whether the human will could fulfill God's commands without the interior gift of grace as Augustine understood it. Pelagius's own views, those of associates such as Caelestius and Julian, and later polemical “Pelagianism” should be distinguished. The term should not be used as a generic insult for strong accounts of choice.

Semi-Pelagianism

“Semi-Pelagianism” is a later label for positions associated with certain fifth- and sixth-century southern Gallic debates, especially the claim that the initial movement toward faith can arise from unaided human will even though subsequent grace is necessary. The Second Council of Orange (529) rejected an unaided beginning of faith. John Cassian's precise relation to the label remains disputed. Synergism as such is not Semi-Pelagianism, because synergistic traditions can affirm that grace initiates and enables every saving response.

Monergism

Monergism means that God alone accomplishes the regenerating or converting act in the relevant salvific sense. It does not entail that people are physically forced or that renewed people never act.

  • Reformed accounts describe effectual calling as renewing the will so that the person comes freely and willingly.
  • Lutheran confessions assign conversion entirely to the Holy Spirit through Word and sacrament, yet differ from Reformed theology on universal grace, election, resistance, baptism, and apostasy.

Thus “monergist” is not a complete soteriology.

Synergism

Synergism means that God's grace is indispensable while the human person genuinely cooperates with grace. It does not mean “God does 50% and humans do 50%,” nor does cooperation earn the prior gift. Catholic, Orthodox, Arminian, Wesleyan, and many other synergistic accounts describe grace as creating, healing, or enabling the very response it calls for.

Prevenient grace

Prevenient grace is grace that precedes the saving response. In Arminian and especially Wesleyan theology, it addresses inability by enabling a response without ensuring that response. Wesley says fallen humanity cannot make even the first saving movement until God acts, then identifies the grace that goes before as “preventing grace” in “On Working Out Our Own Salvation”.

Other traditions also speak of preceding grace without adopting the Wesleyan model. Article X of the Thirty-Nine Articles, for example, uses preventing for grace that gives the good will and working with for grace acting with it.

Effectual or irresistible grace

In Reformed theology the outward gospel call can be resisted, but the inward effectual call given to the elect succeeds. “Irresistible” does not normally mean that God drags a person to faith while leaving the person hostile. The Westminster Confession X describes renewal of the will and a person coming “most freely, being made willing by his grace.”

Critics ask whether such renewal preserves the control required for responsibility; defenders answer that transformed desire is liberation rather than coercion. This is a philosophical dispute in addition to a doctrinal one.

Resistible grace

The question must identify the grace and stage involved:

  • Catholic: grace precedes and enables; a person can resist. Thomist/Bañezian and Molinist schools explain sufficient and efficacious grace differently.
  • Eastern Orthodox: divine initiative and human synergy are affirmed; resistance and apostasy remain possible.
  • Lutheran: unregenerate people resist by nature, conversion is God's work alone, and grace can be rejected; this combination does not fit a simple Reformed/Arminian grid.
  • Arminian/Wesleyan: prevenient grace enables but does not necessitate faith.
  • Reformed: the general call is resistible; effectual calling infallibly brings willing response.

Regeneration and faith

Model Typical account Caution
Reformed Regeneration is logically prior to and produces faith Logical priority need not imply a time gap
Lutheran The Spirit creates faith through the means of grace; baptismal regeneration is integral Not reducible to a Reformed ordo salutis
Arminian/Wesleyan Prevenient grace enables faith; regeneration is associated with believing union with Christ “Faith first” does not mean unaided faith
Catholic Justification includes forgiveness and interior renewal; adults are moved by grace and freely assent Sacramental initiation resists a single abstract sequence
Orthodox New birth, baptism, faith, repentance, and theosis belong to an ecclesial life of grace Often framed as healing and participation rather than an ordered mechanism

Perseverance and apostasy

Perseverance of the saints in confessional Reformed theology means God preserves the truly regenerate to the end. Preservation emphasizes God's sustaining action. Conditional security holds that continued saving participation is conditioned on persevering faith. Apostasy is final repudiation or abandonment of faith, not every serious lapse.

Catholic and Orthodox accounts allow grace to be forfeited; restoration is possible through repentance. Lutheran confessions warn that believers can fall away. Wesleyan and many Pentecostal traditions teach conditional security. The Assemblies of God explicitly rejects unconditional security in its official assurance statement. Baptist traditions vary, though the Southern Baptist BF&M affirms perseverance.

Comparison without conflation

Question Monergism Synergism
Is grace necessary? Yes Yes
Does grace precede saving response? Yes Yes in the traditions surveyed here
Does the person genuinely will and act? Yes, as renewed by grace Yes, enabled and sustained by grace
Is conversion caused by God alone in the defining sense? Yes No; grace-enabled cooperation is real
Is this the same as compatibilism/libertarianism? No No

Confessional Reformed evaluation

The descriptive distinctions above remain the basis for fair comparison. The biblical dossiers set the inability, renewal, resistance, and apostasy passages beside their major competing readings. An explicitly Reformed assessment argues that effectual renewal better integrates fallen inability, willing faith, unconditional election, and perseverance than models in which grace is sufficient but the final differentiating response remains undetermined.
