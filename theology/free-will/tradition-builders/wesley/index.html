John Wesley

John Wesley turns the priority of grace and the reality of response into a practical theology of awakening, justification, assurance, sanctification, and perseverance.

Historical context

Dates: 1703–1791
Tradition: Anglican priest; principal organizer and formative theologian of Methodism
Context: evangelical revival, societies and classes, debates with Calvinist Methodists, Anglican sacramental life

Major works

The Standard Sermons; Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament; journals; “Predestination Calmly Considered”; “A Plain Account of Christian Perfection.”

Anthropology and prevenient grace

Wesley affirms that fallen people are dead in sin and cannot make the least saving movement until God works. He then argues that every person receives some measure of preventing or prevenient grace. This grace awakens conscience and enables response without making the response inevitable.

inability → prevenient grace → enabled response → faith/new birth
                                  ↘ resistance remains possible

The sequence is conceptual. Wesley does not reduce grace to a detachable power possessed independently of the Spirit.

Justification, new birth, and sanctification

Justification is pardon and acceptance by grace through faith; new birth is interior renewal. Sanctification continues grace's transformation of desire and action. Christian perfection is perfect love excluding voluntary sin as a reigning principle, not omniscience, freedom from mistake, or independence from grace.

Election and universal grace

Wesley rejects unconditional reprobation and argues that the grace of God is free for all. Election is conditional and Christ-centered. His polemic is pastoral as well as metaphysical: he fears that unconditional predestination obscures God's universal love and discourages holy response.

Perseverance and apostasy

Wesley teaches that a true believer can make shipwreck of faith. Present assurance is real witness of the Spirit; perseverance remains dependent on continuing, grace-enabled faith and use of the means of grace.

Position across the dimensions

Dimension Wesley
Alternatives Restored for saving response by grace
Fallen ability Total inability apart from grace
Grace priority Universal prevenient grace
Grace resistible Yes
Conversion Synergistic after enabling grace
Election Conditional, in Christ
Apostasy Possible
Modern comparison Libertarian-leaning

Influence

Charles Wesley's hymns, John William Fletcher's polemical theology, and Methodist conferences help consolidate the tradition. Wesleyan teaching shapes the Holiness movement and many Pentecostal streams, though neither later movement simply repeats Wesley.

Common misunderstandings

  • Prevenient grace is not natural moral neutrality.
  • Wesley does not teach salvation by works; even good desire flows from grace.
  • Perfect love does not remove creaturely weakness or the need for atonement.
  • Arminian Methodism is not identical to all Remonstrant theology.

Primary sources

  • “On Working Out Our Own Salvation”
  • “Free Grace”
  • “The Scripture Way of Salvation”
  • Wesleyan / Methodist tradition
