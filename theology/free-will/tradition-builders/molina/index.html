Luis de Molina and Domingo Báñez

The late-sixteenth-century de auxiliis controversy concerned how efficacious grace, providence, predestination, and free choice cohere within Catholic doctrine. Molinists and Thomist/Bañezians are internal Catholic schools, not Catholic versus non-Catholic positions.

Luis de Molina

Dates: 1535–1600
Tradition: Jesuit, Latin Catholic
Major work: Concordia liberi arbitrii cum gratiae donis (1588)

Middle knowledge and freedom

Molina distinguishes God's natural knowledge of necessities, middle knowledge of counterfactuals of creaturely freedom, and free knowledge of the created world God chooses. Before the creative decree, God knows what each possible free creature would do in specified circumstances. By choosing which feasible world to actualize, God exercises meticulous providence without causally determining the libertarian choice.

Molinist freedom requires that, with all prerequisites for action present, the agent retain power for either outcome. The enduring objection asks what grounds counterfactuals about never-actualized free choices and whether their prior truth limits divine or creaturely control.

Grace and salvation

Molina affirms original sin, the necessity and priority of grace, predestination, and Catholic justification. Middle knowledge is not a Pelagian theory of salvation; it is a proposed account of providence and the distribution of grace with free response.

Domingo Báñez

Dates: 1528–1604
Tradition: Dominican, Thomist
Role: Aquinas commentator and major representative in the grace controversy

Báñez emphasizes God's physical premotion and the intrinsic efficacy of grace. God moves secondary causes according to their modes, so the will acts voluntarily rather than by external compulsion. Critics argue the account threatens libertarian alternatives; Bañezians reply that dependence on first causation perfects rather than cancels creaturely agency.

Common misunderstandings

  • Molinism is not simple foreknowledge: middle knowledge is logically prior to the creative decree.
  • Bañezian motion is not described by its defenders as coercion.
  • Catholic teaching did not canonize one school as the only permissible theory.

Reformed Assessment

Molinism's strongest contribution is a technically sophisticated attempt to join meticulous providence to robust libertarian freedom without identifying foreknowledge with causation. From a confessional Reformed perspective, it carries the grounding burden for pre-decretal counterfactuals of creaturely freedom and makes providence depend on truths not determined by God, while its Catholic soteriology differs independently from Reformed justification and perseverance. See Why Catholic Accounts Are Weaker from a Reformed Perspective.
