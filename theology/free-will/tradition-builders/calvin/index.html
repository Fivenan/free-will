John Calvin and Jonathan Edwards

John Calvin supplies a formative Reformed account of providence, voluntary bondage, regeneration, election, and perseverance. Jonathan Edwards later gives one of the tradition's most systematic analyses of will, desire, and necessity.

John Calvin

Dates: 1509–1564
Tradition: French Reformed reformer in Geneva
Major work: Institutes of the Christian Religion

Anthropology and free will

Calvin says fallen people act voluntarily rather than by external compulsion, yet their will is captive to disordered desire and cannot turn to saving good without the Spirit. Responsibility therefore rests on willing action and the creature's corrupt disposition, not a libertarian power of contrary choice.

Providence

Providence encompasses events and creaturely causes. Calvin rejects fate as an impersonal chain and insists that God's governance does not erase the different intentions of agents. His biblical and pastoral vocabulary should not simply be replaced with modern causal determinism.

Grace, election, and perseverance

Regeneration renews mind and will; effectual calling produces willing faith. Election is unconditional and magnifies gratuitous mercy. Calvin treats reprobation within the decree but distinguishes divine righteous purpose from the wicked intentions and culpability of agents. Union with Christ and the Spirit's preservation ground perseverance.

Theodore Beza and Francis Turretin

Theodore Beza (1519–1605) systematizes Reformed predestination in later controversy; historians debate the degree of continuity with Calvin. Francis Turretin (1623–1687) develops scholastic distinctions among necessity, coercion, contingency, and divine concurrence. Neither should be used as a transparent substitute for Calvin's own formulations.

Jonathan Edwards

Dates: 1703–1758
Tradition: New England Reformed/Congregational
Major work: Freedom of the Will (1754)

Desire, necessity, and inability

Edwards argues that the will follows the greatest apparent good or strongest motive as apprehended. Natural inability involves lack of capacity or opportunity and excuses; moral inability is inability arising from contrary inclination and does not excuse, because the act still expresses the agent's heart.

Edwards rejects a self-determining will conceived as choosing its own prior act of choice, which generates a regress. Liberty is doing as one wills without external hindrance, while moral necessity can coexist with praise and blame.

Grace

Regeneration supplies a new spiritual disposition; faith follows effectual grace. This is a recognizably theological compatibilism, though contemporary compatibilists may reject Edwards's metaphysics or theology.

Common misunderstandings

  • Calvin's voluntary bondage is not the claim that desire is externally forced.
  • “Natural ability” in Edwards does not mean moral neutrality or saving ability.
  • Later Reformed compatibilism develops Calvin; it should not be attributed to him word-for-word.

Connection to the Reformed evaluation

Calvin's account of voluntary bondage and providence and Edwards's analysis of moral inability supply important parts of the later Reformed synthesis, but neither thinker is the argument's sole authority. A Reformed Evaluation of Free Will tests their shared model against confessional, biblical, and philosophical criteria and includes its author-of-sin and ultimate-sourcehood difficulties.
