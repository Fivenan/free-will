Martin Luther

Martin Luther's controversy with Erasmus makes the “bondage of the will” central to Reformation debate. His denial concerns unaided power in relation to God and salvation, not the existence of willing or ordinary deliberation.

Historical context

Dates: 1483–1546
Tradition: German reformer; formative Lutheran theologian
Controversy: Erasmus's On Free Will (1524) and Luther's On the Bondage of the Will (1525)

Major works

On the Bondage of the Will; Heidelberg Disputation; Freedom of a Christian; Large Catechism; lectures on Romans, Galatians, and Genesis.

Anthropology and free will

Luther distinguishes matters “below” from matters “above.” Humans choose, plan, govern households, and act voluntarily in temporal affairs. Before God, however, the fallen person cannot produce faith, merit grace, or turn themselves from unbelief. “Bondage” is a directed will captive to sin, not absence of psychological willing.

In arguing against Erasmus's deliberately modest account of free choice, Luther also uses sweeping language about divine foreknowledge and necessity. Interpreters dispute how those arguments relate to his pastoral, Christological, and sacramental theology and to later Lutheran confessions.

Grace, conversion, and salvation

The Spirit creates faith through the gospel. Justification is God's forensic gift received by faith, not the reward for a self-originating decision. The person so renewed trusts and acts willingly; works follow faith rather than cause justification.

Predestination

Luther sometimes speaks with strongly Augustinian predestinarian language, but later confessional Lutheranism directs election toward Christ and the means of grace and rejects symmetrical reprobation. Luther should not be relabeled “a Calvinist” on the basis of overlap.

Philip Melanchthon and Martin Chemnitz

Melanchthon (1497–1560) drafted the Augsburg Confession and its Apology. His later language about three “causes” in conversion has generated debate over development and consistency; it should not be read back into early Luther without argument. Martin Chemnitz (1522–1586) helped shape the Formula of Concord, which gives the mature confessional distinctions on conversion and election.

Common misunderstandings

  • “Bondage” does not mean humans are rocks, robots, or without wills.
  • Luther's debate concerns saving relation to God more narrowly than every action.
  • Lutheran monergism is not identical to Reformed effectual calling and perseverance.
